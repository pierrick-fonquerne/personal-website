The stateful neuron: what a neuron's memory lets it compute.

A perceptron sees the world as a series of unrelated snapshots. At each instant it decides, then forgets everything. A biological neuron, by contrast, remembers the instant before. This page is about a single question, deeper than it looks: what can a neuron that remembers compute, that a memoryless neuron never will? The whole gap between a logic gate and a living cell lies in one word: state. A stateful neuron carries an internal variable that evolves over time and depends on its history. A stateless neuron has only the present instant.

Let us start with the limit. The first formal model of the neuron dates from 1943, with McCulloch and Pitts: a weighted sum of the inputs followed by a threshold. Its decisive property is that its output at one instant depends only on the inputs at that same instant. Nothing else. No trace of the past, no adaptation. Rosenblatt, in 1958, adds the learning of weights, but this does not change the dynamics. The consequence is a strict impossibility: a stateless neuron can represent no function that depends on the order or the delay between its inputs. It is blind to time by construction.

What does the internal state change? In 1907, long before computing, Lapicque proposed the integrate-and-fire model: the neuron accumulates incoming current with a leak, and fires when a threshold is crossed. The state variable is the potential. For the first time, the neuron integrates its inputs over time. In 1936, Hill couples the threshold to the potential: the threshold is no longer a constant, it depends on what the neuron has just experienced. As soon as an internal variable decays over time, the neuron keeps a trace of its recent past. And a trace is exactly what is needed to compare two instants.

The interactive demonstration on the page shows this step by step, with two units that receive exactly the same raw stream. First task, simultaneity: fire if the two inputs arrive together. The stateless neuron succeeds, and this is a fairness control: it proves it is not rigged to lose. Second task, coincidence a few milliseconds apart: the stateless neuron fails, because at the moment of deciding only one input is present, the other already belongs to the past. The trace neuron keeps the first pulse as it decays. Third task, order: fire if A precedes B. The set of inputs is identical, only the order changes, so the memoryless neuron is unable to tell them apart.

Beyond these examples, a whole family of models enriches the internal state. Hodgkin and Huxley, in 1952, describe the action potential with its ion channels. Izhikevich, in 2003, obtains a dozen behaviors with two variables only. Brette and Gerstner, in 2005, add an adaptation variable. On the side of temporal computation, the Jeffress model, in 1948, explains sound localization through coincidence detection, and the Hassenstein and Reichardt correlator, in 1956, explains motion detection through delay lines. And we climb one more rung with the power of a single neuron. Poirazi and Mel, in 2003, show that a pyramidal neuron behaves like a two-layer network. And above all, in 2021, Beniaguev, Segev and London establish the pivotal result of this page: mimicking a single real cortical neuron requires a deep network of five to eight layers, but if the receptors that carry its internal state are removed, a single layer suffices. In other words, the internal state is the computational depth.

All of this rests on one point of honesty: both units must receive the same raw stream, and only the rich neuron is allowed a memory. If we handed the poor neuron a pre-computed temporal variable, it would be us, not it, doing the computation. And the refutation condition remains posed: let a stateless unit succeed at coincidence or order on an equivalent raw stream, and the thesis falls.

Let us pick up the thread. A memoryless neuron does not see time. A stateful neuron does, and Beniaguev gave us the number: its internal memory is worth five to eight layers of a stateless network. Temporal richness is not a biological ornament, it is computational power. One open question remains: if a single stateful neuron already carries such depth, what becomes of a network that learns to exploit this temporal dimension, instead of flattening it? This is the ground of a research program to come, of which this brick is the first foundation.
