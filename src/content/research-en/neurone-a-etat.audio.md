The stateful neuron: what a neuron's memory lets it compute.

A perceptron sees the world as a series of unrelated snapshots. At each instant it decides, then forgets everything. A biological neuron, by contrast, remembers the instant before. This page is about a single question, deeper than it looks: what can a neuron that remembers compute, that a memoryless neuron never will? The distinction seems thin, but it is not. The whole gap between a logic gate and a living cell lies in one word: state. A stateful neuron carries an internal variable that evolves over time and depends on its history. A stateless neuron has only the present instant.

Let us start with the limit. The first formal model of the neuron dates from 1943, with McCulloch and Pitts: a weighted sum of the inputs followed by a threshold. Its decisive property is that its output at one instant depends only on the inputs at that same instant. Nothing else. No trace of the past, no fatigue, no adaptation. Rosenblatt, in 1958, enriches this scheme with the perceptron, which learns its weights, but the learning concerns the weights, not the dynamics. The consequence is a strict impossibility: a stateless neuron can represent no function that depends on the order or the delay between its inputs. If two scenarios present the same inputs at the same instant in a different order, it produces exactly the same output. It is blind to time by construction.

What does the internal state change? The idea is older than one might think. In 1907, long before computing, Lapicque proposed the integrate-and-fire model: the neuron accumulates the incoming current in a membrane capacitance with a leak, and emits a pulse when the potential crosses a threshold. The state variable is the potential. For the first time, the neuron integrates its inputs over time. In 1936, Hill went further by coupling the threshold to the subthreshold potential: this is accommodation. The threshold is no longer a constant, it depends on what the neuron has just experienced. As soon as an internal variable decays over time, the neuron keeps a trace of its recent past. And a trace is exactly what is needed to compare two instants. Time stops being a backdrop, it enters the computation.

The interactive demonstration on the page shows this step by step, with two units that receive exactly the same raw stream of two inputs. First task, simultaneity: fire if the two inputs arrive at the same instant. The stateless neuron succeeds, and this is a fairness control, it proves it is not rigged to lose, that it is competent on memoryless tasks. Second task, coincidence a few milliseconds apart: the stateless neuron fails, because at the moment of deciding only one input is present, the other already belongs to a past it cannot see. The trace neuron keeps the first pulse as it decays, and the sum crosses the threshold. Third task, order: fire if A precedes B, stay silent if B precedes A. The set of inputs is identical, only the order changes, so the memoryless neuron is unable to tell them apart, while the trace neuron, with asymmetric delays, manages it.

Beyond these examples, a whole family of models enriches the internal state. Hodgkin and Huxley, in 1952, describe the action potential of the squid giant axon with four coupled equations and three gating variables for the sodium and potassium channels. Izhikevich, in 2003, produces several distinct firing patterns, bursting, frequency adaptation, post-inhibitory rebounds, with just two variables. Brette and Gerstner, in 2005, add an adaptation variable and reproduce more than a dozen behaviors observed in vivo. On the side of temporal computation, the Jeffress model, in 1948, explains sound localization through coincidence detection between the two ears. The Hassenstein and Reichardt correlator, in 1956, explains motion detection through delay lines, the memorized delayed signal creating the temporal asymmetry that encodes direction. And we climb one more rung with the power of a single neuron. Poirazi, Brannon and Mel, in 2003, show that a pyramidal neuron behaves like a two-layer network thanks to its dendrites. And above all, in 2021, Beniaguev, Segev and London establish the pivotal result of this page: mimicking a single real cortical neuron, to the millisecond, requires a deep network of five to eight layers, but if the NMDA receptors that carry its internal state are removed, a single layer suffices. In other words, the internal state is the computational depth.

This story continues today. Since 2020, spiking networks can be trained despite the non-differentiability of the spike, thanks to surrogate gradients, formalized by Neftci, Mostafa and Zenke in 2019. Local, spike-timing-dependent plasticity is reconciled with backpropagation through the e-prop work of Bellec and colleagues, in 2020. And on the hardware side, neuromorphic processors such as Loihi, by Davies and colleagues in 2018, use energy only at the moment of a discharge. Intel's Hala Point system, in 2024, pushes the idea to one billion one hundred and fifty million neurons for two thousand six hundred watts, where the human brain has eighty-six billion neurons for around twenty watts.

All of this rests on one point of honesty that must be faced: both units must receive the same raw stream, and only the rich neuron is allowed a memory. If we handed the poor neuron a pre-computed temporal variable, it would be us, not it, doing the computation of time, and the comparison would be rigged in its favor. The refutation condition therefore remains posed, in black and white: let a stateless unit succeed at coincidence or order on an equivalent raw stream, and the thesis of this page is false.

Let us pick up the thread. A memoryless neuron is bounded, it does not see time. A stateful neuron does, and Beniaguev gave us the number: its internal memory is worth five to eight layers of a stateless network. Temporal richness is not a biological ornament, it is raw computational power. One open question remains, which this page does not claim to settle: if a single stateful neuron already carries such depth, what becomes of a network that learns to exploit this temporal dimension, instead of flattening it as the dominant architectures do? This is precisely the ground of a research program to come, of which this brick is the first foundation.
