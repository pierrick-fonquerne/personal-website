Local learning: learning without a global gradient.

Let us start with a gap that should stop us. Training a large model costs on the order of a megawatt over weeks. The human brain, by contrast, reasons, learns continually and generalizes on about twenty watts. That gap of five to six orders of magnitude is not a hardware detail: it points to a different computational principle. At the heart of the difference, the artificial neuron is stateless. It is a weighted sum, effectively a logistic regression. Depth creates non-linearity, not richness. And the whole network is trained by one global mechanism, backpropagation. The question of this piece fits in one sentence: can we learn without propagating error backward?

To grasp what is at stake, we need to see why backpropagation cannot be a local rule. Three locks forbid it by construction. The first is weight transport. The backward pass reuses the forward weights, transposed. To compute its error, a neuron would need to know the weights of the synapses downstream of it, which makes no biological sense: a synapse does not read the synapses of its neighbors. The second lock is update locking. No layer updates until the full backward sweep completes. The whole network advances in lockstep, in a global lock. The third lock is the two separate phases. Activity must first flow forward, then error must flow backward, and these two phases never overlap. The lesson is sharp: backpropagation is powerful, but it is non-local by construction. That is precisely what a local rule must give up.

So if giving up backpropagation costs so much, why attempt it? Because locality promises four things the global mechanism cannot offer. First, energy proportional to real activity: only the units that fire spend, instead of a systematic global sweep. Second, continual learning: no frozen phase, the network adapts on the move, without separating training and inference. Third, biological plausibility: a local rule resembles what living systems actually do. And fourth, co-location of memory and computation: the synapse is both where you store and where you compute, which removes the costly back-and-forth between memory and processor. The cost is clear: today, no local method matches backpropagation at scale. So this is a research bet, not a solved problem.

Let us now look at three concrete paths. The first is Forward-Forward, proposed by Hinton in 2022. The idea is to replace the forward plus backward pair with two forward passes. The first processes positive data, meaning real data, the second processes negative data, meaning corrupted data. Each layer locally raises a goodness on positive data and lowers it on negative data. There is no backward pass, no global error: each layer learns from its own local information only. To picture it, imagine running a positive pass and then a negative pass, and watching each layer adjust on its own, sending nothing back. The limit is real: the architecture stays fixed, the synapses stay scalar, and the method remains below backpropagation on benchmarks. But it proves that a deep network can learn without ever sending error back.

The second path flips the perspective: it is predictive coding, studied notably by Millidge, Tschantz and Buckley in 2022. Here, each layer predicts the activity of the next one and locally minimizes its prediction error, that is, the gap between what it observes and what the layer above predicted. These researchers showed that this approach can approximate backpropagation along arbitrary computation graphs, using only local error units. It is currently seen as the most promising energy-based alternative to backpropagation.

The third path comes straight from biology: it is STDP, spike-timing-dependent plasticity. It is a biologically documented local rule, and its principle is simple. The weight change depends only on the relative timing of the two spikes. If the pre neuron fires before the post neuron, the synapse strengthens: this is potentiation, because the pre took part in the causality of the following spike. If the post fires before the pre, the synapse weakens: this is depression. A third factor, a reward or a neuromodulator, can gate consolidation: this is three-factor learning. Diehl and Cook, in 2015, combined STDP with winner-take-all inhibition to recognize digits with no backpropagation at all. To picture it, imagine sliding the timing difference between the two spikes, and watching the synapse strengthen or weaken depending on the direction. And this path connects directly to my own work: it is exactly the kind of local rule sought by the SOAG program, and the criterion that could steer the mutations described in the structural plasticity concept.

We can formalize all of this in a few words, with no symbols at all. Backpropagation computes the gradient of a weight as the product of a local error term and the input. But that error term is itself a sum over every unit downstream. In other words, the update of a single weight depends on the entire rest of the network. A local rule, by contrast, replaces that global term by a signal available at the synapse itself: a function of the two activities it connects, plus an optional global modulator, such as a reward. The whole debate is right there: can a local rule match the global gradient?

Let us sum up the comparison. Compared with backpropagation, Forward-Forward, predictive coding and STDP each drop at least one lock: local learning, no backward pass, or biological plausibility. But none of these approaches yet matches backpropagation at scale.

So we should stay honest about the real state of the field. Local methods still underperform backpropagation on large benchmarks. We lack a theory saying which local rule yields which capability. Comparing these methods is hard, because they optimize different objectives. And the open question remains: is locality a constraint to overcome, or the key to efficiency?
