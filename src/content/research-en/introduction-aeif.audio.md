AEIF: an intelligence born from its own experience.

A child does not read ten billion words to learn what fire is. It reaches out toward a flame, feels the pain, and builds knowledge anchored in its own lived experience: timestamped, emotional, sensory, irreducibly its own. AEIF asks a simple, vertiginous question: could an intelligence be built that way too, out of what it lives rather than out of statistics over corpora?

Let us start with the problem posed by large language models. They are impressive, but their intelligence rests on a particular bet that we have to face squarely. They predict tokens, not concepts: they estimate the most likely next word, without necessarily manipulating the idea behind it. Once trained, they are frozen: their weights no longer move, they no longer learn from their interactions. Their personality is simulated by a prompt: we dictate a role to them, they did not become it. And they have no body and no grounding: their words are tied to no sensory experience, and lacking any grip on the real, they hallucinate. Hence the thesis that guides all of AEIF: intelligence is born of lived experience, not of statistics over corpora.

The heart of AEIF is a shift in perspective. Event sourcing, usually a mere software pattern, becomes here a cognitive mechanism. Each experience becomes an immutable, timestamped, typed event. That event never disappears: it is stored in episodic memory, it consolidates semantic memory, it modifies the neural network, it colors the emotional state, and it reinforces or weakens personality traits. The unique payoff of this approach: you can replay the formation of a belief, a trait, a fear. The why of a behavior stops being a black box. It becomes traceable.

Imagine you could scrub through a timeline, event after event, watching the memory of an agent that discovers fire. The trait fear of fire was written nowhere. You would see it emerge slowly, solely from the accumulated events: remove them, and the trait fades. Nothing is hard-coded, everything is lived.

AEIF is organized into six layers that communicate only through events. None knows the inside of the others: they share only a bus and a protocol. From the bottom up. The first layer carries the shared event protocol and bus, the foundation of everything else. The second is from-scratch neural compute in Rust, with dynamic topology and Hebbian learning. Then come perception and the motor layer: the senses and the body, the interface with the world. Then memory, which gathers episodic, semantic, procedural and emotional memories, with consolidation, selective forgetting and associative recall. Above that, cognition: reasoning, planning, attention, emotions, motivations and decision. And at the very top, identity: traits, preferences, values, self-awareness and narrative continuity. Emit a sensory event, and you would watch it climb the layers, transformed at each stage.

How does such an architecture learn? Through predictive coding, the idea championed by Friston. The cycle is clear: predict, observe, compare, then learn from the error. When surprise is low, the observation confirms the model, which is reinforced. When surprise is high, the observation contradicts the model, which must change, and the network is modified. The crucial point: the learning signal is intrinsic surprise, not an external reward as in reinforcement learning. The agent does not wait to be praised, it corrects itself. Set the observation far from the prediction to trigger high surprise, then iterate several times, and you would watch the gap shrink as the model moves toward the real.

The AEIF network has no fixed shape. It practices neuroplasticity: neurons and synapses are created when new concepts appear, reinforced by repetition following Hebb's rule, pruned by disuse. It is not only the weights that change: it is the topology itself that evolves. This is where AEIF meets another of my research programs. AEIF provides the substrate: a network where every structural mutation is an immutable, traced, replayable event. But its mutations stay random: it knows how to mutate, not yet how to choose. The missing engine, those local rules that decide what to grow and what to let die, is the subject of the SOAG program. This is exactly the hinge of the shared concept of structural plasticity.

We can state things cleanly, without symbols. A lived event is a triple of a timestamp, a type, and a payload. The agent state at a given moment is the fold of its whole event history, folded from an initial state. And learning is driven by surprise, the gap between what the agent predicts and what it observes. When that gap is small, the model is confirmed and reinforced. When it is large, the network must change.

AEIF is not alone in seeking a grounded intelligence. Placing it against two other families brings out its own bet. Compared with large language models, trained on massive text corpora, and with designed world models like JEPA, which learn in a self-supervised way on video and physics, AEIF bets on lived events. Where the former rest on frozen pre-training and the latter on a carefully designed hierarchy, AEIF learns continuously, by experience, on dynamic networks coded from scratch. Its memory is neither a mere context window nor a generic persistent store: it is event-sourced and multi-type. Its personality is not simulated by a prompt, it emerges from experience. And its grounding is neither absent nor purely physical: it is sensorimotor and emotional. Each bet is coherent. AEIF's is the least explored of the three.

It would be dishonest to present all this as solved. The open work is plentiful. Representation, first: neural only, or hybrid neural-symbolic? Quantifying surprise: which metric holds, and over which space? Forming hypotheses: how does the agent generate them, and how does it test them? Consolidating and forgetting: when to engrave a memory, when to let it fade? What to predict, and when: over which modality and at what horizon to carry the prediction? Grounding concepts: how to tie an abstract idea to the experience that founds it? Time and causality: how to reason about the order and the causes of events? And finally the emergence of personality: how does a trait really arise from accumulated events? This is the final goal, and it remains open.
