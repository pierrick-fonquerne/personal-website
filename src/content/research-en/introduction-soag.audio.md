SOAG: intelligence as self-organization, not optimization.

Early flight imitated the flapping wings of birds. They were ornithopters that ran on brute force and superficial mimicry. Efficient flight came only much later, once we identified the principle of lift: a fixed wing, aerodynamics, something that owes nothing to copying biology and everything to understanding why it works. Today's large language models may still be in the flapping-wings phase. They work, yes, but by brute force, stacking parameters the way we once stacked engine power onto inefficient designs. The motive question of this program is simple, and it fits in a single sentence: what is the lift principle of intelligence?

The founding hypothesis also fits in one sentence, and everything else follows from it: the brain computes only what is necessary, where it is necessary, when it is necessary. Sparsity, spikes, locality, plasticity, inhibition and oscillations are not independent mechanisms. They are all consequences of this single principle. A large language model does the exact opposite: it computes everything, everywhere, all the time. To grasp the gap, picture a grid of cells. In the dense mode, every cell lights up at every step: the whole network is paid for at all times. In the sparse mode, only a few cells in a small neighborhood light up: you pay only for what is stimulated. Let both modes run and watch the cumulative cost diverge. At first the gap seems modest, then it widens step after step, until it becomes a chasm.

History clarifies this bet, and it reads as a story of bifurcation. In nineteen forty-three, McCulloch and Pitts proposed the logical, stateless neuron: a gate that sees the present and nothing else. In nineteen forty-nine, Hebb formulated his rule, neurons that fire together wire together. In nineteen fifty-eight, Rosenblatt built the perceptron, a machine that genuinely learns. Then, in nineteen sixty-nine, Minsky and Papert proved that the perceptron cannot learn XOR, and that result triggered the first AI winter. The field then split into several paths. The first response was to add layers: that is backpropagation in nineteen eighty-six, then deep learning in twenty twelve, then large language models. The second response was to enrich the component itself: that is spike timing dependent plasticity in nineteen ninety-eight, self-organized spiking networks, and this program. A third response, a later one, was to design a world model, the path taken by LeCun with JEPA. The motive question of this piece is therefore the following: is the first response the only one possible, or just the easiest given the tools available in nineteen eighty-six?

The first axis is to enrich the component. A poor neuron, in the McCulloch and Pitts sense, is stateless: it sees only the present instant, and the same input always yields the same output. A rich neuron instead carries internal state that unfolds over time: a membrane potential, a leak, an adaptive threshold, a reserve of energy. The same train of inputs produces, in the rich neuron, a temporal integration that the poor one simply cannot express. To feel it, imagine injecting pulses too weak to fire on their own. The poor neuron never reacts, because it forgets each pulse the moment it arrives. The rich neuron, on the other hand, accumulates pulse after pulse, its potential rises, until the moment it crosses its threshold and finally discharges.

The program explores six complementary axes. The first four form the irreducible core, what I call version zero. Axis one: redefine the neuron as a unit with rich internal state, with potential, adaptive threshold and energy, not as a mere weighted sum followed by a nonlinearity. Axis two: redefine the synapse as the main locus of processing, a typed synapse with its own temporal dynamics, governed by purely local learning rules. Axis three: make inhibition a first-class computational mechanism, needed for sparsity, competitive selection and dynamic stability. Axis four: the stability mechanisms, local constraints such as an energy budget, homeostasis, mortality and forgetting, which prevent divergence without any global control. Then come two extensions. Axis five: an emergent spatial organization, a functional space where distance shapes connectivity and where clusters form out of activity. Axis six: a multi-scale temporal dynamics, where each component has its own time constant, from the spike that lasts a millisecond to structural reorganization that unfolds over hours or days.

Here is the link with my other work. This program looks for the missing engine of structural plasticity: the local rules that decide what to grow and what to let die, with no central control. The relationship is sharp. AEIF provides a traceable substrate that mutates at random, and this program aims to be the criterion that orients those mutations. This engine is exactly the hinge described in the cross-cutting concept of structural plasticity.

We can state all of this cleanly, without symbols. A local learning rule depends only on the relative timing of two spikes, that is on the time separating the instant when the receiving cell fires from the instant when the sending cell fired just before it. Stability, in turn, comes not from a global error signal but from a local energy budget: each unit spends to fire and must stay under its budget. The sentence that captures the spirit is simple: nothing here reads a global error, every decision is local.

What this program draws no longer looks like a neural network in the classical sense. It looks instead like a computational ecosystem: a distributed, event-driven adaptive system with a plastic topology and local regulation. Four implications follow. Intelligence becomes an emergent behavior, rather than an optimized function. Architecture becomes a result, rather than a choice made in advance. Time becomes the very medium of computation, rather than a mere dimension of representation. And memory becomes a dynamic, rather than a storage. The program explores how far this metaphor can go, and where it stops being productive.
