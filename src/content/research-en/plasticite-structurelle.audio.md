Structural plasticity: when the network rewrites its own architecture.

A human brain does not merely tune the strength of its connections. It grows new ones, destroys others, and lets neurons be born and die throughout life. Its shape is in motion. A classical artificial neural network, by contrast, receives its architecture once and for all before training, and never touches it again. In this piece, we explore what we gain, and what we still fail to understand, when we make the shape of the network itself something that learns.

Let us start with an essential distinction. The word plasticity actually covers two very different mechanisms. Synaptic plasticity, first: the strength of an already existing connection, its weight, changes. This is exactly what backpropagation does, adjusting numbers on a frozen graph. Structural plasticity, second: the graph itself changes. Neurons and connections are created and removed. Topology stops being scenery and becomes a variable. The first answers the question: how much. The second answers the question: who talks to whom. This whole piece is about the second.

This structural plasticity is not an engineer's oddity. It is the normal regime of living systems. The brain constantly forms new synapses, that is synaptogenesis. It massively eliminates others, especially in adolescence, when a large share of childhood synapses disappears, that is synaptic pruning. The brain sculpts itself by removing as much as by adding. New neurons even appear in adulthood, notably in the hippocampus. And there are critical periods, windows where topology is especially malleable before it settles. The lesson is sharp: a good architecture is not laid down in advance, it is found through growth and destruction, guided by activity.

Why make the shape learn? For three reasons. First, continual learning: a network whose shape can evolve does not need to be frozen after training, it keeps adapting as its world changes. Second, efficiency: if structure is built only where activity justifies it, the network uses resources only where they are useful, instead of computing everything everywhere all the time. Third, self-organization: shape becomes a result, rather than a choice imposed in advance. You no longer draw the architecture, you define the rules that make it emerge.

But there is a trap, and it is the heart of the matter. Picture two networks starting from the same point and undergoing structural mutations. The first keeps every mutation at random. The second keeps only those that improve a performance measure. The first wanders forever, never making progress. The second climbs quickly. That is the whole difference between shaking a structure and making it learn. Mutating is not learning. What makes the difference is a criterion that steers the changes. This criterion can be a global selection pressure, as in an evolutionary algorithm. Or a local rule, like spike timing dependent plasticity in biology, where a connection strengthens when it helps trigger the activity of its neighbor. Remember this idea: structural plasticity is only worth the criterion that guides it.

Growing is only half the story. The other half is removing. A dense network holds many nearly useless connections. Removing the weakest ones lightens computation without breaking the function. This principle has a name in recent research: dynamic sparse training. It reveals that a well chosen subnetwork, sometimes called a lottery ticket, reaches the full network's performance with only a fraction of its connections.

None of this is speculative. Several families of work make it concrete. Neuroevolution, with the NEAT algorithm, evolves both weights and topology through selection. Dynamic sparse training, with methods like SET and RigL, starts sparse, then drops and regrows connections by usefulness. More radically, the work of Barland and Gil obtains generalization, and even the grokking phenomenon, from purely local rules, with no global error function at all. Other approaches, such as self motivated growing networks or structural plasticity in spiking networks, decide insertion and pruning from local statistics. The common thread: topology can become a locally governed variable, not a template imposed from the outside.

We can state all this cleanly. A network is a graph evolving over time. Structural plasticity is four operations on this graph: add or remove a neuron, add or remove a connection. A local growth rule can rely on activity correlation, because two units that fire together gain from connecting. A pruning rule, on the contrary, removes a connection whose weight stays negligible. And the whole thing reads as a joint optimization of function and form, under a sparsity pressure that pushes the network to stay sparse, hence efficient.

This concept is the hinge between two of my research programs. AEIF provides the substrate: a network where every structural mutation is an immutable, traced, replayable event. But its mutations stay random, it knows how to mutate, not yet how to choose. SOAG, on the other hand, looks for the missing engine: the local rules that decide what to grow and what to let die, with no central control.

None of this is solved. Stability remains a challenge, because a topology that changes constantly can diverge or forget everything. Scaling too, because current demonstrations stay on small problems. The theory is incomplete, and comparing networks of different shapes is delicate, because we no longer compare only numbers, but shapes.

One last, more personal note. I am not a researcher by trade. This concept is precisely the one that made me feel the difference between building a tool and understanding a principle. My framework worked, it added and removed neurons without flinching, and yet it learned nothing. It was evenings spent watching a graph mutate for nothing that pushed me toward the literature on local learning. Sometimes, seeing clearly what does not work is the best way to guess where to look next.
