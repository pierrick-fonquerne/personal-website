Late generalization: when the network understands long after memorizing. Grokking, or how a network goes from perfect memorization to sudden generalization, and what that says about our datasets.

A neural network memorizes all its training examples, stays at zero on unseen examples for thousands of epochs, then suddenly starts to generalize. This phenomenon has a name, grokking. In this piece, we look at what it is, why it matters for a program on continual and local learning, and what we still do not know.

The phenomenon was described in twenty twenty-two by Power and colleagues, on small algorithmic tasks, including modular addition, a calculation where you keep only the remainder of a division. The network quickly reaches one hundred percent on training. Validation stays at the floor for a very long time, then jumps. We can tell four behaviors apart: comprehension, where training and validation rise together; grokking, where validation rises well after training; memorization, where validation never rises; and confusion, where nothing rises.

A September twenty twenty-six paper, by Tian, Al-Ars, Kitsak and Hofstee, argues that this delay comes first from how the data is cut between training and validation. Their test is to keep the same network, the same optimizer and the same number of examples, and to change only the split. When the pairs a, b and b, a always fall on the same side, validation never takes off. Since addition is commutative, the second pair teaches almost nothing new. Their underlying idea is that what matters is not the number of examples, but the useful information they carry. On this reading, grokking would be a warning sign, that of a dataset too poor for the model, rather than a hidden feat. Be careful: this is a hypothesis, not an established fact, because the proposed cause is not measured directly.

Why does this matter for continual and local learning? A system that learns continuously has no fixed split, but it does have an order in which examples arrive, and the same question applies: how much useful information does each example bring? A stream gives few examples per unit of time, so we may spend a long time in the regime where memorizing and generalizing compete. The authors also argue that low-loss regions depend on the network and the data, not on the optimizer. If that is true, the same split should also block a network trained by local rules, without a gradient. That is a testable prediction, and it is what ties this concept to my programs.

Several questions remain open. Does the result hold with weight decay, the force that pulls weights toward zero? The paper does not test it on its blocked case. What remains beyond a transformer and modular arithmetic? Nothing is tested on other architectures or on real data. Does a local rule behave like the gradient when facing the same split? How can we measure the misalignment of the regions directly, instead of inferring it from observed behavior? And what becomes of the split when examples arrive one by one, in an order that matters?

In one sentence. Grokking is generalization that arrives very long after memorization, and a serious lead says it depends mostly on how the data is split, which asks a direct question of any system that learns from a stream.
