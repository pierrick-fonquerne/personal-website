Research journal. Remembering without storing: memory as a landscape. Four September papers, and one shared idea about how a network keeps a trace.

Within a few days in September, four papers from four different teams told me the same thing. A network does not only keep a trace by filing it somewhere. It also keeps it by changing the shape of its own dynamics. When an idea comes back from four directions like that, it deserves more than a margin note, so I am writing it down here.

The first paper comes from Javadzadeh, Hennequin and colleagues, in Nature Neuroscience. They record two mouse visual areas at the same time, area V one and the lateromedial area, called L M, and they perturb them with optogenetics, that is with light, to test causality. What they see is that the reciprocal connections between the two areas slow down the decay of the activity patterns that encode the stimulus the same way on both sides, and speed up the decay of the others. My reading: the network chooses what to keep by tuning decay rates, not by copying a value.

The second paper comes from Vedovati and colleagues, who build a network where neurons and astrocytes, those glial cells long seen as mere support cells, work together. In this network, reward triggers a bifurcation, a switch that moves a stable attractor to another region of state space. Two mechanisms combine. On one side, uncertainty about the context builds up until the dynamics switch. On the other, the attractor is shallow, with a depth set by the entropy of the environment, and that is what gives behavior a kind of viscosity.

The third paper is about juvenile zebrafish, again in Nature Neuroscience. Learning an odor shifts and stretches the manifold of that odor, meaning the shape its activity takes in neural space, inside the homolog of the olfactory cortex. The model that comes with the study draws a sharp line. Well balanced assemblies of excitatory and inhibitory neurons partly generalize to a related odor, while an attractor regime does not generalize. My reading is that the attractor always completes toward the learned state, so it crushes exactly the variation that would have carried generalization.

The fourth paper is theoretical. Vaidya uses a mean-field theory to describe what happens to a recurrent network while it learns. Feedback grows up to a critical strength, and it drives the network from a chaotic regime to stable dynamics specific to the task. The edge of chaos then stops being a setting you pick once and for all: it is a crossing the network goes through, with a critical time you can compute.

So what does this change for my living and persistent neuron? The journal and the signature remain the core of the model. But the question is no longer only where the trace is kept: it is also what shape the dynamics took. So I will measure decay rates, attractor shifts and distances between manifolds. And one safeguard comes before any conclusion. McKenzie proposes to separate three things that are often confused: a mere change of gain, a displacement within the manifold, and a true displacement off the manifold, and the paper states when they can be told apart. Without that test, saying the neuron persists stays impossible to tell apart from saying it amplifies.

One question remains open. The zebrafish result suggests the useful regime sits just before the attractor. What I still need to find out is whether a more expressive neuron stays there more easily than a network whose topology was designed by hand. That is my next experiment, on a reservoir first.

In one sentence. Four papers from four teams show that a network remembers as much by reshaping its dynamics as by storing, and that shape is what I will now measure, with a test that tells true persistence apart from mere amplification.
