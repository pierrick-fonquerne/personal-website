Research journal. Motifs and hubs: a counterpoint to my page. A reading note on a simulation paper, and on what it changes in how I think about the shape of a network.

I read a paper by Jagdev, Bertram and Yu, published on September thirtieth on arXiv. I read it because it touches my core question: does the shape of the wiring matter, and where does it come from?

The authors simulate four networks of one thousand spiking neurons. The background is either random or hub-based, meaning a few very well-connected neurons. Motifs, small circuits of two to four neurons, are inserted or not. The control networks have the same number of synapses, so only the layout changes. An intermediate level of noise makes the activity the most regular, and that is called coherence resonance. Motifs add eleven percent of regularity on a random background, and only three percent on a hub background. The hub background dominates: its signal-to-noise ratio is about seventy-two, against twenty-six. It also responds faster and stronger, with a peak at fifty milliseconds and forty-seven hertz, against one hundred milliseconds and twelve hertz. Finally, removing the seven point five percent most connected neurons drops that ratio from twenty to eleven, while removing the same number of random neurons leaves it around seventeen.

This paper is a counterpoint to my page on structural plasticity. There I write that topology is only worth the criterion that steers it, and that a template imposed from outside is not the goal. Here, the authors impose motifs and hubs by hand, and the gain is already clear, with no learning at all. So the starting shape weighs more than my page lets on. I also keep that the layout matters, not only the strength, because shuffling the connections of a small two-neuron motif drops the ratio from twenty-four to eighteen. And the hub brings back the stability problem: what makes the strength also makes the fragility. The authors point to the next step themselves, motifs and hubs that emerge through plasticity, and that is exactly my subject.

Now, what I don't buy. It is a preprint, so not peer reviewed, and everything is simulated, with no biological data. The testbed is small, one thousand neurons, and even five hundred for the rewiring test, with strengthened motifs to make the effect visible. The effect of motifs stays modest. Plasticity is active in the simulations, but its role is never measured. Above all, nothing links this rhythm to an ability to learn, because the paper measures regularity and signal transmission, nothing else. The code is not published, and a few parameters are missing to reproduce it.

Still open. I first want to reproduce the ranking of the four networks in Rust, then rerun without plasticity, which is the first measurement the paper does not contain. Then I will start from a random network without motifs, add creation and removal of synapses, count the motifs and follow the signal-to-noise ratio over time. Will hubs appear? I don't know, and that is what makes the experiment interesting.

In one sentence. Wiring imposed by hand already changes a network's rhythm a lot, which nuances my page, but the paper says nothing about learning, and it is up to me to test whether motifs and hubs can emerge through plasticity.
