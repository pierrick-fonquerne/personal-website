Chapter two. The membrane potential. Where the leak factor we set by hand in chapter one comes from.

In the previous chapter you gave the neuron a memory with a single knob: the retention factor lambda, slid by hand between zero and one. It worked, but it fell out of the sky. Why zero point seven, and not something else? What does it actually stand for? This chapter opens up the cell to show that lambda is not an arbitrary setting: it comes straight from the physics of a membrane, a tiny capacitor draining through a resistor.

Let us start by looking at the membrane as two electrical parts. A real neuron bathes in a fluid full of charged ions. At rest, its membrane holds a small, constant voltage difference between the inside and the outside of the cell, on the order of minus sixty-five millivolts: this is the resting potential, the value the cell drifts back to when left alone. This membrane is a very thin fatty wall separating two salty solutions. It piles up opposite charges on either side, exactly like a capacitor, a component that stores charge. The larger its capacitance, written C, the more charge it takes to raise its voltage. But the membrane is not sealed: it is pierced by tiny pores, the ion channels, which let a trickle of current leak through. This leak behaves like a resistor, written R: the larger it is, the slower the charge escapes. Hold on to these two roles, because the whole chapter lives in their meeting: the capacitance stores, the resistance lets things leak.

Put these two parts together, and you get the RC circuit, the simplest electrical model of a passive membrane, one that merely leaks. Charge the capacitor up to a starting voltage, then leave it alone. Current escapes, the voltage drops. How fast? Here is the decisive intuition: the leaking current is stronger the higher the voltage. The more charge remains, the faster it escapes; the less remains, the slower the leak. A quantity whose rate of decrease is proportional to its own value decays as an exponential. The discharge therefore follows: V of t equals V zero times e to the power minus t over tau, where tau, the Greek letter, is the product R times C. The voltage at time t is the starting voltage multiplied by e raised to the power minus t over tau.

This product tau has a very concrete meaning. Look at the voltage at the moment t equals tau itself: V of tau equals V zero times e to the power minus one, that is about zero point three seven times V zero. After a duration tau, the membrane has lost about sixty-three percent of its charge; thirty-seven percent is left. This is why tau is called the time constant: it measures the characteristic duration of the leak. A small time constant, and the memory evaporates in a flash; a large one, and the membrane holds the trace of what it received for a long time. This is the exact physical translation of what the lambda knob was tuning in chapter one. And since tau is the product R times C, two very different membranes can share the same clock: doubling the resistance or doubling the capacitance lengthens tau the same way.

Then comes the heart of the chapter. We have two descriptions of the same leak. Physics describes it continuously: at every instant, the voltage is V zero times e to the power minus t over tau. Chapter one described it in small steps, with the input-free recurrence: v subscript t plus one equals lambda times v subscript t. Do they describe the same thing? To compare, let us sample the continuous curve, that is, look at it only every delta t. At instant k times delta t, the continuous law gives V zero times e to the power minus k delta t over tau, which, by the rule of powers, is V zero times, e to the power minus delta t over tau, all raised to the power k. Now set lambda equals e to the power minus delta t over tau. Then the sampled value is V zero times lambda to the power k, which is exactly the sequence produced by the chapter-one recurrence. The conclusion is strong: the chapter-one recurrence is not a crude approximation of the physics, it is its exact sampling, provided you choose lambda equals e to the power minus delta t over tau. The leak factor set by hand had a hidden formula, and here it is. By the way, since delta t and tau are positive, the exponent is negative, so lambda always lands between zero and one: it is a retention, never an amplification.

On the page, a component lets you see all this. An initial charge sets the potential, then the membrane leaks. The green curve is the continuous discharge, the violet dots are the recurrence values, sampled every delta t. First set the resistance and the capacitance, and watch the time constant tau equals R times C appear while the slope changes. Notice that the curve always crosses the thirty-seven percent line right at the instant tau. Then play with the step delta t: the violet dots spread apart or close up, but they always stay sitting exactly on the green curve. That is the theorem, before your eyes.

And what about the real neuron? The RC circuit describes a passive membrane: it stores, it leaks, and that is all. Yet a real neuron actively builds its spike. In nineteen fifty-two, Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley measured, on the squid giant axon, how certain ion channels open and close depending on the voltage itself, creating the chain reaction that generates the spike. Their model, awarded the Nobel Prize in nineteen sixty-three, adds to the plain RC some nonlinear conductances. The RC is not wrong then, it is incomplete: it is the foundation on which the following chapters add just enough nonlinearity.

In one sentence. The neuron's leak is anything but arbitrary: the membrane is a capacitor draining through a resistor with a time constant tau equals R times C, and sampling that continuous discharge every delta t gives back exactly the factor lambda equals e to the power minus delta t over tau, set by hand in chapter one.

The chapter ends with three paper-and-pencil exercises, solved line by line: computing a time constant, finding the time after which only half the charge is left, and recovering the leak factor from tau and the step.

Towards chapter three. We have tied our recurrence to the physics of a leaking membrane, but two pieces are still missing: the equation that governs the voltage when an input current arrives continuously, and the firing mechanism, the threshold and the reset. Chapter three writes the membrane's differential equation, adds a threshold and a reset, and obtains the full integrate-and-fire model, of which our recurrence was only the sampled, input-free version.
