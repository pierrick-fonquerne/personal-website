Chapter one. The neuron that forgot time. Why the foundations neuron cannot hear time, and how an internal state gives it that ear back.

A barn owl hunts in complete darkness. It does not see the mouse darting through the leaves: it hears it. The sound reaches its nearer ear a few millionths of a second before the other, and that tiny gap is enough for it to strike precisely. Somewhere in its brain, neurons are measuring a time difference on the order of a microsecond. Now take the neuron from the foundations course, the one that computes y equals f of, w dot x plus b. Could it do what the owl does? The answer is no, and the reason is precise. This chapter starts from that impossibility to rebuild a neuron that can, in fact, hear time.

The foundations neuron lives in an eternal present. Go back to its central formula: for an input x, it produces y equals f of, w dot x plus b. Look at what is in this equation, and above all at what is not. There are the inputs, the weights, the bias, the activation. There is no trace of the moment when the input arrives. You can present x today or tomorrow, the result is the same. The neuron has no clock, no memory of what happened just before. We say it is stateless: its output depends only on the present input. If you feed it a sequence of inputs, one per time step, it processes them one by one, independently. Each output only looks at its input of the moment. The past never enters the computation. An eternal present, with no before and no after. And this is not a flaw: it is what makes classical deep learning simple and massively parallel. The flip side is that for anything intrinsically temporal, a sound, speech, a gesture, you have to be clever.

The owl's problem is to hear a time gap. Two signals arrive, one per ear. What carries the information is neither the intensity of the left sound nor of the right one taken alone: it is the time gap between the two. The brain must fire strongly when the two signals arrive almost together, and stay silent when they are too far apart. This ability has a name: coincidence detection, proposed as early as nineteen forty-eight by Lloyd Jeffress as a model of sound localization. Why does the stateless neuron fail? Suppose the two inputs never land on exactly the same time step. Then, at each step, the neuron sees only one active input. Since its output depends only on the present, it reacts the same whether the gap is tiny or huge. It is missing a place to hold the trace of the first input while waiting for the second.

Let us give the neuron a state. The idea that unlocks everything: add an internal variable that remembers, for a brief moment, what just arrived. We call it the membrane potential, by analogy with the electric charge that builds up on the membrane of a real neuron. Let us write it v. This variable obeys three rules, at each time step. It leaks: with no input, v slowly falls back toward zero, keeping only a fraction lambda of its value, lambda between zero and one. It integrates: when an input arrives, its charge adds to v. And it fires: if v reaches a threshold theta, the neuron emits a pulse and v resets to zero. The first two rules fit in a single recurrence: v at step t plus one equals lambda times v at step t, plus the input at this step. The new value of the potential is the old one times the retention lambda, plus the input received. The factor lambda is the neuron's memory. At lambda equals zero, it forgets everything instantly and becomes stateless again. The closer lambda gets to one, the longer it holds the trace of the past. This three-rule neuron is the simplest spiking neuron: the integrate-and-fire model, introduced by Louis Lapicque as early as nineteen oh seven. A neuron whose output depends on an internal variable evolving over time is a stateful neuron.

Coincidence detection comes directly out of the leak. Look at two inputs of the same charge c, separated by delta steps. The first carries the potential to c. Over delta steps with no input, it leaks down to c times lambda to the power delta. Then the second adds c. At the moment of the second input, the potential is therefore c times, one plus lambda to the power delta. If delta is small, lambda to the power delta is close to one, the potential approaches two c: the two charges add up almost entirely, the threshold is crossed. If delta is large, lambda to the power delta tends to zero, the first input has evaporated before the second arrives, and the neuron stays silent.

On the page, an interactive component lets you manipulate all of this. Two inputs of fixed charge arrive, the second shifted by a delay you set. You watch the potential rise at the first, leak, then rise again at the second, and cross or not the threshold drawn as a dashed line. Start from zero delay, watch it fire, then raise the delay step by step until the neuron falls silent: you have just measured its coincidence window. Then play with the retention: a membrane that holds its charge better forgives larger delays, while a higher threshold demands a tighter coincidence.

The spike is a language in time. The stateful neuron no longer returns a continuous number like the sigmoid of the foundations. It returns a pulse, a brief all-or-nothing event: it fires, or it does not. Hence a new question: where does the information hide in a train of pulses? In rate coding, what matters is the number of spikes per second, their rate. In temporal coding, what matters is the precise instant of each spike. The owl uses the second route: it is the arrival time, not the rate, that carries the position of the prey. Temporal coding lets you compute with very few spikes, hence very little energy.

In one sentence. The foundations neuron is memoryless and therefore blind to time; giving it a membrane potential that integrates, leaks and fires turns it into a stateful neuron able to detect the coincidence of two signals, and thus to hear time.

The chapter ends with three paper-and-pencil exercises, solved line by line: integrating the potential by hand for three different delays, proving that a stateless neuron is blind to temporal shift, and computing the coincidence window from the firing condition.

Towards chapter two. We spoke of the membrane potential as a variable that rises and leaks, settling for a retention factor set by hand. But where does that leak actually come from? In a real neuron it has a physical cause: the membrane behaves like a small capacitor discharging through a resistor. Chapter two opens the cell to find the concrete origin of the retention, before chapter three turns our recurrence into the differential equation of the full integrate-and-fire model.
