Foreword. Why give time back to neurons, what this course brings, and how to read it.

The course on the foundations of neural networks showed you a powerful brick: the neuron that computes a weighted sum of its inputs, runs it through a non-linear function, and returns an output. That brick has a quiet but deep flaw. It has no memory. It receives, it answers, it forgets. Time does not exist for it.

This course fixes that flaw. We are going to give neurons back the dimension they were missing: time. You will meet another family of networks, spiking neural networks, where each neuron carries an internal state that evolves, accumulates, and fires discrete pulses. Closer to biology, far more energy-frugal, and carrying a way of computing that classical networks cannot easily imitate.

To place what we study, a classic grid, due to Wolfgang Maass in nineteen ninety-seven, sorts networks into three generations. First generation: the binary threshold neuron of McCulloch and Pitts, an all-or-nothing output. Second generation: the continuous-activation neuron, sigmoid or ReLU, the one that underpins all of modern deep learning and the whole foundations course. Third generation: the spiking neuron, whose output is no longer a number but a train of pulses placed in time. That is the subject of this course. Maass proved that a network of this third generation can, with fewer neurons, compute functions that earlier generations need more resources to approximate. The promise is there as early as ninety-seven. The difficulty of training it, though, took decades to tame.

A word on prerequisites. To follow, you need the artificial neuron and its weighted sum, the activation function, and the idea of gradient descent, all covered in the foundations course, which is the natural prerequisite for this one. You do not need detailed neurobiology or formal differential equations: we introduce them step by step. The course aims at an advanced undergraduate level while staying readable for a motivated reader coming from the foundations.

The path follows a simple thread: give time back to the neuron, make it compute, then make it learn, and finally make it run on dedicated hardware. A first block gives time back to the neuron, with the membrane potential and the integrate-and-fire model. A second block shows how to code information in spikes and how a synapse transmits a signal that lasts. A third block tackles learning, from temporal plasticity to the surrogate gradient. A final block descends all the way to silicon, neuromorphic computing and its chips. On the page, a diagram sums up these four blocks and their progression. This course is written chapter by chapter: block one starts now, the later ones will arrive as the writing progresses.

In one sentence. Spiking neural networks give the neuron back the dimension of time: their output is no longer a frozen number but a train of spikes, and it is that difference this course will unfold, from the basic model to silicon.

Towards chapter one. It all starts from a concrete frustration. A barn owl locates a mouse in pitch darkness thanks to a gap of a few microseconds between its two ears. The foundations neuron cannot do this, and for a precise reason. That is where chapter one begins.
