Chapter six. Making the index durable. An index that lives in memory vanishes on restart: how do you write it to disk once and for all, without ever corrupting it, even if the power dies mid-write?

In the previous chapter, we earned the right to trust an index: the differential oracle certifies that it really retrieves the neighbors it claims to return. But this carefully built and verified index lives in memory, and memory is wiped at the slightest restart. Rebuilding an index of several million vectors takes hours of computation. So the question of this chapter is down to earth, and yet it decides whether the engine survives: how do you write this index to disk once and for all, read it back intact, and survive a power cut that happens in mid-write? This is a change of worlds. Until now we reasoned in geometry, in distances, in recall. Now we reason in bytes, in crashes, in guarantees. And we are about to meet a sneaky threat, a direct cousin of the one from chapter five: a saved index can read back without the slightest apparent error, and still lie about its contents.

An in-memory index is volatile by nature. Cut the power, kill the process, restart the machine: everything is lost. For a state to survive, you have to make it durable, meaning you write it to a medium that keeps its value without power, a disk or a flash drive, and you make sure the write truly reached that medium. You might think this is trivial: just open a file and dump the index into it. That is exactly where the trap hides. Writing data takes time, and during that time, anything can happen. The machine can fall in the middle of the operation. And an interrupted write does not leave a nice empty file: it leaves a file that is half written.

Imagine your index occupies ten blocks on disk. You launch the save, which rewrites those ten blocks one by one. The machine shuts off after the sixth. On disk, you now have six fresh blocks and four old blocks, welded into the same file. This is a torn write, and it is the nightmare of all persistence. The trap is not data loss, because loss we know how to handle: just rebuild everything. The trap is silent corruption. This hybrid file opens without error. Its header is consistent, its first records read perfectly. It looks valid. But its internal pointers cross the border between old and new, and the index we read back is an incoherent monster that will return absurd neighbors without ever raising an exception. You recognize the pattern from chapter five: what looks correct on the surface can be wrong underneath.

You might think that a single call to fsync, at the end, is enough. The fsync does force data from the volatile caches down to the physical disk. But it says nothing about what survives if the crash happens before that fsync. As long as the write targets the final file directly, an early crash leaves a mix. Durability is not obtained by forcing harder: it is obtained by writing differently.

Against the torn write, engineering has invented several remedies, each making a different trade-off between safety, write cost, read speed, and freshness. The first, naive one is in-place overwrite: you rewrite the final file directly. Fast and simple, but it is precisely the one that tears. The atomic write by rename fixes it: you write the new index into a temporary file, beside the original, without touching it; once the temporary is complete and forced to disk, a rename publishes it all at once. The snapshot does the same with a complete, periodic photograph of the state. The append-only log never rewrites anything: it appends each change to the end of a file, so a crash can only damage the last record, which is truncated on read. And the memory-mapped file lets the operating system manage the back and forth between memory and disk, elegant, but with durability that depends on when the system decides to flush its pages.

Of these remedies, one deserves a pause, because it is the most universal: the atomic rename. Its idea has three steps. First, you write the entire new index into a temporary file, distinct from the official one; during all that time, the official file stays the old index, complete and intact, so a crash here only loses the half-written temporary, with no consequence. Then you call fsync on the temporary to guarantee it is truly on disk, and not just in a cache that the crash would erase. Finally, you rename the temporary over the official one. That last gesture is the secret: at the filesystem level, the rename is atomic. There is no instant when the index name points to a half-replaced file. At every moment it designates either the whole old file or the whole new one. The half-written state is made impossible by construction. Write into the temporary, then fsync the temporary, then rename over the official: that is what text editors that never corrupt your file, and most serious tools, do under the hood.

Now suppose the index is correctly written, without any crash. Are you safe? Not yet, because there is a second way to lie, unrelated to crashes: serialization itself can betray you. To serialize is to turn an in-memory structure into a sequence of bytes for the disk; to deserialize is the inverse. We would like these two operations to be exact inverses: reading back what you just wrote should return the starting state, to the byte. But that is easy to break. A vector of floats written with too little precision comes back rounded, hence different. A set of neighbors written in an unstable order comes back in a changed order, hence different bytes from one write to the next. Hence the round-trip test: you serialize the state, you read it back, and you demand that the result be identical to the original. It is, word for word, the differential oracle of chapter five, transposed to persistence. The reference is no longer the slow exact index, it is the in-memory state. And the trap from chapter five returns: a logical check, which verifies that you have the same nodes and the same neighbor sets, stays green even when the neighbor order has changed, because logically it is the same. Only a byte-for-byte comparison against a canonical reference catches that instability. The logical check is blind where the strict oracle sees.

In the component, you bring the two dangers together. At the top, choose a persistence strategy and slide the crash moment along the write plan, to observe what the read-back recovers depending on where the machine falls: with in-place overwrite, a crash in the middle leaves a corrupted file, whereas with atomic rename, at the same point, the old index stays intact. At the bottom, the round-trip oracle: turn on the serialization bugs and watch the logical check stay green while the byte-for-byte comparison turns red. A deeper rule runs through all of this: on a real engine, you do not write your own crash-safe format by hand. You lean on a proven transactional layer, a key-value store that already implements atomicity and durability, then you validate it with a round-trip test. That is far cheaper than debugging a corrupted index in production.

The chapter ends with two paper-and-pencil exercises, solved line by line: walk one crash through three strategies to see which one tears, then show why the logical check lets through a neighbor-order bug that the byte-for-byte oracle catches.

In one sentence. An in-memory index only becomes durable once written to disk, and writing it naively over the old file exposes it to the torn write, a silent corruption that you neutralize through atomicity, write beside then rename all at once, or only append to the end of a log; and as in chapter five, only a byte-for-byte round-trip oracle guarantees that what you read back is exactly what you had in memory.

Towards chapter seven. The index is now durable and verified: it survives restarts and we can prove we read it back faithfully. But all the search we have built rests on meaning, captured by vectors, and there are queries where meaning is not enough: an exact reference number, a rare proper noun, a keyword that must appear verbatim. Chapter seven therefore marries our vectors to the lexical world, the world of exact words, to build a hybrid search that looks both by meaning and by the letter.
