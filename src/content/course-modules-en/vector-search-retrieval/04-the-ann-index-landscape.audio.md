Chapter four. The landscape of ANN indexes. Four index families, three riches you can never keep all at once.

In the previous chapter, HNSW handed us a small miracle: finding a nearest neighbor in about log n hops, instead of scanning the n vectors one by one. But we closed the chapter on a confession: it is superb, but it is neither free nor unique. The hierarchy of graphs devours memory, updating it or filtering its results is delicate, and above all, other ways to avoid the full scan exist, making entirely different trade-offs. This chapter takes a step back: rather than diving into yet another algorithm, we draw the map of all its cousins, and give ourselves a grid to choose.

A single question guides us, and it is uncomfortable. When you index millions of vectors, you covet three things at once. Recall first: actually retrieving the right neighbors, without missing any, exactly the measure from chapter two. Latency next: answering fast, by touching as few vectors as possible. Memory last: fitting the index into available RAM, knowing that an embedding vector of dimension fifteen thirty-six already weighs six kilobytes, and that at a hundred million documents you are talking hundreds of gigabytes. Here is the cruel law of this field: you never maximize these three riches together. It is a triangle whose only one side you can occupy at a time. Gaining speed means giving up touching everything, hence risking missing some. Saving memory means compressing, hence losing precision. The word ANN, Approximate Nearest Neighbor, names this deliberate surrender: an approximate search is not a failed search, it is a search that has chosen its battle.

To avoid getting lost, a very simple grid suffices, built on two independent questions. First question: how do you avoid scanning everything? Three answers. Do nothing special and go through it all, that is the flat index. Carve the space into cells and visit only the most promising, that is partitioning. Or link the vectors with a graph and navigate it, that is HNSW's approach. Second question: how do you store and compare the vectors? Two answers. Keep them in full precision, exact but heavy. Or compress them into tiny codes, light but approximate. These two axes are orthogonal: you combine any answer from one with any answer from the other. That is what explains the proliferation of real indexes.

Let us tour the four landmark families. The flat index first. It does nothing to avoid the scan: it compares the query against all vectors, in full precision. Its recall is perfect, by construction, but it touches the n vectors on every query and stores them all in the clear. It is the worst for speed and memory, the best for quality. Hence its true role: we keep it as the oracle, the ground truth against which we measure the recall of the others.

IVF next, the inverted file. The first real idea for going faster: do not search everywhere. We carve the space into cells, like a city into districts, and file each vector into the district of its nearest center, by k-means. At search time, we compare the query only to the district centers, then scan only the residents of the few nearest districts. This count of visited districts is called nprobe. With a small nprobe, we answer fast but risk missing a neighbor hidden elsewhere, so recall drops; by raising nprobe, recall climbs back until it becomes exact again. IVF buys speed with a little recall, but does not touch memory: the vectors stay in the clear. IVF wins latency, not RAM.

Product quantization, finally, attacks the opposite axis: memory. What if, instead of storing each vector in full, we summarized it with a few bytes? We split each vector into slices, and for each slice we learn ahead of time a small dictionary of typical pieces, a codebook. A vector then becomes just a sequence of indices: for each slice, the number of the most similar typical piece. Where a vector weighed six kilobytes, its few codes fit in about ten bytes: we divide memory by a hundred or more. The price reads in the word quantization: the distances are now only estimated, so recall drops. And beware the trap: alone, product quantization gains no speed. We still scan the n codes, one by one, each comparison having merely become very cheap. It wins memory, not latency. Line them up: Flat is exact but slow and heavy, IVF wins speed, HNSW wins it even better but pays edges in memory, and PQ wins memory but still scans everything. None grabs all three.

In the component, you can see these four families genuinely measured on a cloud of clustered vectors: each family is a point in the recall-latency plane, and the size of its bubble tells its memory. Turn the knobs and watch the points slide along their trade-offs, without any of them reaching the ideal corner, high recall, few comparisons and a small bubble all at once.

The most beautiful part is that these bricks combine, precisely because the two axes are independent. The most widespread index at very large scale, IVFPQ, partitions the space for speed and compresses the vectors for memory: it wins two sides of the triangle at once, sacrificing more recall. The reading grid is therefore not a shelf of rival products, but a box of Lego. And when the index no longer even fits in RAM, at the scale of a billion vectors, a family apart, of which DiskANN is the known representative, keeps most of the graph on disk and brings into RAM only the strict minimum: it is the on-disk corner of the landscape, where you trade latency for the ability to host a gigantic index.

The chapter ends with two pencil-and-paper exercises, solved line by line: placing three indexes on the triangle from their spec sheets, then choosing a family to index eight hundred million vectors on a memory-tight machine, where quantization becomes mandatory and marries partitioning.

In one sentence. No index maximizes recall, latency and memory all at once: Flat is exact but heavy and slow, IVF buys speed by partitioning, HNSW buys it even better but pays in memory, product quantization buys memory but still scans everything, and real indexes combine these bricks according to the side of the triangle they accept to sacrifice.

Towards chapter five. One word kept coming back without our daring to look it in the eye: we measure the recall of an approximate index by comparing it to the exact oracle. But that oracle, who provides it, and at what cost? Chapter five builds this judge: the differential oracle, the test bench that pits the fast index against the slow truth, measures recall and latency side by side, and turns the choice of an index into a quantified decision. It is the climax of the course, where we stop taking an index at its word.
