Chapter seven. The lexical side, B M twenty five and hybrid search. Semantic search, brilliant on paraphrases, sometimes misses an exact invoice number, and here is how to reconcile the two worlds.

In the previous chapter, we made the index durable and verified. All of our search now rests on a solid foundation, but it rests on one single thing: meaning, captured by vectors. And there are queries where meaning alone is not enough. Picture your document base, and this query: invoice eight thousand eight hundred and thirty one. You are not looking for a document that vaguely talks about billing, you want exactly that invoice. To a semantic engine, that number is just one point among others, a neighbor of eight thousand eight hundred and thirty, and of two thousand two hundred and seven. It hands you a pile of invoices that all look alike, with no way to tell the right one apart. Search by meaning has just hit what it cannot see: the letter.

We could go back to keyword search, but it has its own blind spot. Ask it to cancel a payment, and it will never find a document titled refund procedure for an order, because not a single word is shared. This is the vocabulary mismatch: two texts can talk about the same thing using entirely different words. So we have two symmetric blind spots. The semantic side excels on synonyms but drifts on exact identifiers. The lexical side pins the exact word but stays deaf to synonyms. The idea of this chapter is to refuse the choice and make the two collaborate. That is hybrid search.

But before marrying two scores, we need a good lexical score. That is the job of B M twenty five. The starting intuition is simple: the more a query word appears in a document, the more relevant that document is. We call this count the term frequency. But this raw intuition leads to three traps, and B M twenty five corrects all three.

First trap: not all words are equal. The word the appears almost everywhere and discriminates nothing, while the word consulting is rare and very informative. So B M twenty five weights each term by its rarity, through the inverse document frequency. A term present in few documents gets a large weight, a term present everywhere gets a tiny one. The rare word speaks loudly, the common word stays quiet.

Second trap: the tenth occurrence is not worth the first. A document that contains a word five times is more relevant than one that contains it once, but it is not five times more relevant. The gain must taper off. So B M twenty five saturates the term frequency, at a speed set by a parameter called k one.

Third trap: long documents cheat. A very long text is mechanically more likely to contain any given word. B M twenty five corrects this bias with a length normalization, tuned by a parameter called b. At equal frequency, a document longer than the average is penalized. The usual values are k one equals one point five, and b equals zero point seven five.

We can now produce two scores for the same document: a lexical B M twenty five score, and a semantic score from cosine similarity, the one from chapter one. The temptation is to add the two, and that is a trap. The two scores do not live on the same scale. Cosine is bounded between zero and one. B M twenty five is not bounded, it routinely reaches two, ten, fifteen. Adding zero point nine to fourteen gives fourteen point nine: the cosine is drowned, it weighs nothing anymore. The naive sum does not really fuse, it lets the louder signal crush the other.

The fix is almost insolent in its elegance: if the problem comes from the scales, then let us stop using the scores, and use only the ranks. Whether you are first with a cosine of zero point nine nine, or first with a B M twenty five of fourteen, you are first, full stop. This is reciprocal rank fusion. For each document, we add, over each ranking, one divided by k plus the rank, with k classically fixed at sixty. Being well ranked gives a big contribution, being poorly ranked a small one. The beauty is that we never read a raw score. Multiply every B M twenty five score by a thousand, the ranks do not move, so the fused result does not move either. Rank fusion is scale invariant by construction.

In the component, you can place three rankings side by side on a small corpus: the lexical one, the semantic one, and the hybrid one. You switch between the two queries, and above all you switch the fusion method between rank and naive sum. On the identifier query, you will see B M twenty five isolate the right invoice decisively, while cosine hesitates between three nearly tied invoices. And on the paraphrase, you will see the lexical side get fooled by an intruder, the cosine aim true, and above all the naive sum keep the wrong document on top while rank fusion restores the right one.

The chapter ends with two step by step worked exercises: an inverse document frequency calculation that shows why a rare term weighs about eight times more than a frequent one, and a numeric comparison between naive sum and rank fusion on the paraphrase query.

In one sentence. Search by meaning and search by the letter have symmetric blind spots, we reconcile them with a hybrid search, provided we fuse not the raw scores, whose scales are incompatible, but the ranks, through reciprocal rank fusion, scale invariant by construction.

Towards chapter eight. We now know how to retrieve the right documents, by meaning, by the letter, and by their robust fusion. But retrieving is not answering. The final chapter wires our search engine to a language model: we give it, in context, the best passages surfaced by hybrid search, and it composes the answer grounded on them. This is the architecture called retrieval augmented generation, RAG, where everything we have built finally comes together.
