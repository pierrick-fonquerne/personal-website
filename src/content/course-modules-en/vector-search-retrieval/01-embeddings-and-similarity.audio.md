Chapter one. Embeddings and the geometry of similarity. Meaning as a position in space, and three ways to measure how close two meanings are.

The foreword made a promise, to search by meaning and not by characters. But a promise is not a method. If meaning must become a position in space, two questions arise. What exactly is a position, and how do we measure that two positions are close, given that a perfectly aligned but distant word might lose to an approximate but very nearby one. This chapter answers both.

Let us start with the founding idea, which is older than modern computing. The linguist John Firth summed it up in nineteen fifty-seven with a famous phrase: you shall know a word by the company it keeps. Cat and feline appear in the same contexts, surrounded by the same neighbors, so they have a similar meaning. This idea is called the distributional hypothesis, and it is what makes semantic search possible. To turn it into numbers, we hand the work to a neural network, exactly the building block from the foundations course. By processing large amounts of text, the network learns to assign to each word, to each sentence, an embedding: an ordered list of numbers, that is, a vector, chosen so that similar meanings receive similar vectors. We start from that result: text goes in, a vector comes out.

One important note. In this chapter, vectors live in the plane, in two dimensions, so that we can draw them. Real embeddings have hundreds, even thousands of dimensions, often seven hundred sixty-eight or one thousand five hundred thirty-six. Everything we will see generalizes word for word to those high dimensions, except for one surprise we keep for the end.

We have two vectors, and we want a number that says how close they are. There are three measures, and the choice between them changes who wins. The first is the dot product: you multiply the two vectors coordinate by coordinate, then add everything up. It is large when the two vectors are both long and pointing the same way, and it is zero when they are perpendicular. The second measure follows from the length of a vector, its norm, which is the square root of the sum of the squares of its coordinates, that is, the Pythagorean theorem in n dimensions. From it comes the Euclidean distance, the length of the segment separating the two points: the smaller it is, the closer the points. Watch the direction here, small means close, the opposite of the dot product.

Euclidean distance has a flaw for comparing meanings: it mixes direction and length. Yet for a text, it is often the direction that carries the meaning. So we want a pure angle measure, and that is cosine similarity: the cosine of the angle between the two vectors. You compute it as the dot product divided by the product of the two lengths. The result lives between minus one and one. It is one when the vectors point the same way, zero when they are perpendicular, minus one when they are opposite. In the first component, you can move two arrows and watch the dot product, the norms, and the angle change together. Stretching one arrow without rotating it makes the dot product climb while the angle stays put, and that is exactly the difference cosine erases.

Here is the moment that matters. As long as you compare two vectors, the choice of measure seems academic. But as soon as you rank several candidates against a query, the measure decides the ranking, and two measures can crown two different winners. In the second component, a query faces several candidates ; you can move the query, choose the measure, and toggle normalization, and the ranking recomputes live. You will see one candidate that is aligned but long, and one that is close but tilted, swap places depending on whether you measure by angle or by distance.

Why does normalization reconcile everything. Normalizing a vector means bringing it to a length of one without changing its direction. Place yourself on the unit sphere, where every vector has length one. Expanding the algebra, the squared distance between two such vectors equals two minus two times the cosine. So the cosine equals one minus half the squared distance. This relation says it all: on the unit sphere, the smaller the angle, the smaller the distance, in a strictly monotone way. The two measures then produce exactly the same ranking. This is why many vector databases normalize embeddings on ingestion: they use Euclidean distance, simpler to index, while reasoning in effect on angles. In the engine that serves as the running thread for this course, the proximity measure is not hardwired, it is a parameter, precisely because the two orders coincide after normalization.

One last word, and it is a warning. All our intuition comes from the plane. It will betray us in the next chapter. In high dimension, if you draw two vectors at random, they are almost always nearly perpendicular, and the distances all look alike. The space becomes strangely empty and uniform. This phenomenon is called the curse of dimensionality, and it alone decides the difficulty of all vector search.

The chapter ends with two pen-and-paper exercises, solved step by step: a cosine similarity computed by hand, and a demonstration that cosine and distance disagree when the vectors are not normalized.

In one sentence. An embedding places meaning in a geometric space, and the measure you choose to compare those positions, dot product, distance, or cosine, decides the ranking ; for the meaning of a text, it is the angle that matters, which normalization makes explicit.

Towards chapter two. We now know how to represent meaning as a vector and measure proximity between two vectors. But a real engine has millions of them, and scanning them all for every query is costly. Worse, the intuition of the plane breaks down in high dimension. Chapter two faces both walls: the cost of exact search, and the curse of dimensionality.
