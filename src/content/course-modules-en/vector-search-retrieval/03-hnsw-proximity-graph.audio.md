Chapter three. HNSW, navigating a proximity graph. How to find the nearest neighbor in a few hops, instead of comparing the query against millions of vectors.

In the previous chapter, we hit two walls. Comparing the query against every vector gives the exact answer, but that scan costs on the order of n times d, impractical over millions of vectors. And high dimension blurs the very notion of closeness. We ended on a promise: what if the vectors were linked by a network of shortcuts, so that starting from any point and always moving toward a neighbor closer to the query, you reach the goal in just a few hops? This idea has a name, HNSW, and it is today the most widely used index in vector databases.

Let us start by changing our point of view. Until now, the database was a bag of vectors with no relationships: to answer, we were forced to touch them all. Let us give them links. Each vector knows a handful of close neighbors, and only those. We get a proximity graph: the points are nodes, and an edge connects two points judged to be close. The analogy is a social network. You do not know the eight billion humans, just a few dozen close contacts. Yet to pass a message to a stranger on the other side of the world, you hand it to the contact who seems closest to the recipient, who passes it on, and hop by hop the message arrives.

The movement rule is disarmingly simple. You stand on a starting node. You look at the distance from the query to each of its neighbors. You jump to the neighbor closest to the query. You repeat. You stop when no neighbor is closer to the query than the node you are already on. This is greedy search: at each step, the best local move, never looking back.

With every jump, you get strictly closer to the query, so the walk must terminate. The question is not whether it ends, but where. And here comes an unpleasant surprise. The walk stops as soon as you reach a node whose neighbors are all farther from the query than itself. Nothing guarantees that this node is the true nearest neighbor of the whole database. It may be a mere local minimum: a hollow you can no longer descend from with the available links, even though a much better point exists elsewhere, out of reach. Greedy does not mean optimal.

How do you escape a hollow? By not putting all your eggs in one basket. Rather than keeping only the best current node, you keep a list of the ef best candidates seen so far, and you keep exploring their neighbors as long as one of them can still improve the list. That number ef is the beam width. With ef equal to one, you recover greedy search and its traps. With ef equal to two, you keep a backup path: if the best route hits a local minimum, the second candidate can go around the obstacle. The larger ef, the less you err, but the more you compute. This is, intact, the tension from the previous chapter. Unlike exhaustive search, which is exact, HNSW can miss the true neighbor. It is a deliberate trade: you swap a little accuracy for an enormous amount of speed, and you measure what you lose with recall at k.

There remains the deep question: why are a few hops enough? If the graph linked each point only to its immediate nearest neighbors, then to cross the cloud you would have to traverse the whole space one small step at a time: a path as long as the cloud is wide. The solution comes from social networks. In the six degrees of separation experiment, two strangers picked at random on Earth are linked by a chain of barely six relationships. How, with only close friends? Because a few long-range links exist: a friend who moved to the other side of the world. A network that combines many local links with a few long-range links is called a small-world network. The effect is spectacular. On a purely local graph, the number of hops to cross grows like the size of the network. With well-placed shortcuts, it grows only like the logarithm of that size. Going from n to log n means going from a million hops to about twenty.

It remains to build such a graph, and its hierarchy of layers. HNSW builds it incrementally, points inserted one by one, each choosing how high it climbs. When a new point arrives, it is assigned a random level, following a very unbalanced law: almost all points stay at level zero, the base layer, and ever rarer are those that climb high. Concretely, the level equals the floor of minus the logarithm of a uniform draw, times a constant. Since that logarithm is almost always small, the level is almost always zero. It is the rare points that climb high that become the relays of the upper layers. Once its level is known, the point is inserted into each layer up to that level, and connected to its M nearest neighbors, M being the number of connections per node. To wire it in the right place without comparing everything, we use the very search we just learned: HNSW uses its own navigation to build itself.

The real algorithm adds three refinements that keep the graph alive. First, it does not simply connect a new point to its M closest neighbors as found, because they might all be on the same side and turn the point into a dead end. Instead, it selects neighbors for their diversity of directions, which keeps the graph connected and navigable. Second, the base layer, where the fine-grained search happens, allows twice as many neighbors per node as the upper layers, because that is where precision matters most. Third, alongside the ef used at search time, there is a second parameter called efConstruction, which is the beam width applied during insertion: the higher it is, the better the graph, but the longer the build. That cost is paid once, at construction, and must not be confused with the ef you can tune on every query.

Let us stack these layers. At the very top, a handful of points linked by long jumps. Lower down, more points and shorter jumps. At the very bottom, the base layer, which holds all the points. The search exploits this hierarchy like a parachute descent. You enter through the single point at the summit. In that very sparse layer, a few long greedy jumps quickly bring you to the right region. You then drop one level: the best point found becomes the entry point of the layer below, denser, where shorter jumps refine the position. You repeat down to the base, where you widen the beam to ef to pick out precisely the nearest neighbors. It is the idea of a skip list, carried over not to a sorted line but to a space of vectors. Hence the full name: Hierarchical Navigable Small World, the hierarchical navigable small-world graph.

In the component, you can build an HNSW over a small two-dimensional cloud, with its stacked layers, place the query, send the search down layer by layer and watch the path being drawn. Always compare the neighbor found against the true nearest neighbor that the oracle marks for you: that is your recall, live. Try ef equal to one and count how often the descent misses the true neighbor, then raise ef and watch the error vanish.

HNSW is essentially tuned with two numbers, and their roles must be kept distinct. M is the number of neighbors per node, fixed when you build the index. The larger M, the more richly connected the graph, so the higher the recall, but the more memory the index consumes, since all those edges must be stored. M is paid in RAM, once and for all. ef is the beam width at search time: you can change it on every query without rebuilding anything. The larger ef, the higher the recall, but the slower each query. ef is paid in time. The right way to tune HNSW: first choose M according to the memory available, then raise ef until you reach the target recall, while watching latency.

The chapter ends with two pen-and-paper exercises, solved line by line: trace a greedy navigation over a chain of five points and observe that it takes as many hops as the cloud is wide, then decide, when memory is saturated, whether to turn the M knob or the ef knob.

In one sentence. HNSW links the vectors into a hierarchical small-world graph, where a simple greedy navigation, widened by an adjustable beam width ef, finds the nearest neighbor in about log n hops instead of n, at the cost of a recall you choose to tune between memory, through M, and time, through ef.

Towards chapter four. HNSW is beautiful, but it is not the only way to avoid the full scan, and its hierarchy of graphs is expensive in memory. Other index families make entirely different trade-offs: partitioning the space into cells, or compressing the vectors. Chapter four lays out the landscape of approximate nearest neighbor indexes and gives a reading grid to choose according to what you have: memory, speed, or recall, because you can never maximize all three at once.
