Chapter two. Broker versus log. We finally open the mailbox that carries our messages, and we discover there are two very different ways to build it.

In the previous chapter, we learned to name what we send each other: commands, queries, events. But we treated the box that carries them as a black box. Take the scenario again: your order service publishes the event Order paid. What becomes of that message once dropped off? Is it handed to a reader then destroyed, or kept and re-readable later? And above all, who remembers what has already been read: the system, or the reader? The answer to that question splits the whole messaging world into two families.

Let us start with an image. Picture a mailman first. You hand him a letter, he carries it, the recipient signs, and the mailman throws away his copy. His job is done, he keeps no trace. If someone else wanted to read that letter tomorrow, it is too late, it was delivered once then forgotten. Now picture a large bound ledger lying on a table. Every message that arrives is copied onto the next numbered line, and nothing is ever erased. Anyone can sit down, read at their own pace, and go back up. Two readers move independently, and the ledger does not remember who read what: it is each reader who remembers where they are. The mailman is the message broker, whose archetype is RabbitMQ. The ledger is the message log, whose archetype is Kafka.

Let us look at the broker more closely. It manages queues. You publish a message, it files it, it hands it to a consumer, and as soon as that consumer confirms it processed it, the broker erases it. It is gone, no one will re-read it. And if several consumers plug into the same queue, the broker serves them in turn: each message goes to only one of them, and the work splits itself. This is called competing consumers, perfect for a pool of workers charging payments, where each payment must be processed only once. The crucial point is that the information about what remains to process lives in the broker, not in the reader. The direct consequence: a consumer that arrives too late will never see the messages already delivered and erased, because they no longer exist anywhere.

Now the log. It is a sequence of messages written one after another, and never erased: you only ever append to the end. Each message, as it enters, receives a permanent position number, the first at position zero, the next at position one, and so on. To read, a consumer keeps a cursor: the number of the next message it plans to read. This cursor is called the offset, and here is the pivot of the whole chapter: the offset belongs to the consumer, not to the log. The log need not know who has read up to where, it is each reader who holds its offset. From there everything follows. Because the message stays and the offset lives in the reader, a newcomer can reset its offset to zero and re-read the whole history. And several readers move independently. They are gathered into consumer groups: within a group you share the work as with the broker, but two distinct groups each have their own offset, so each reads the whole stream. Three groups plugged into the log means three complete and independent readings of the same stream. One honest nuance, though: never erased has a practical limit, called retention. You keep messages for a duration you configure, and beyond that, the oldest eventually get recycled.

The real difference, as you now understand, is not a queue versus a sequence of lines. It is where the read position lives. On the broker side it lives in the broker, which erases after acknowledgement, so one reader and no replay. On the log side it lives in the consumer as an offset, on a kept message, so several groups and replay possible. Neither is better, they answer two different needs.

In the interactive component, you can run the same stream through both models, side by side. Produce a few messages, then deliver them on the broker side while watching the queue empty, and advance the offsets on the log side while watching that nothing is erased. And above all, make the revealing move: add a late reader. On the broker side it faces an empty queue, it has nothing to read. On the log side it starts from offset zero and re-reads the whole history. That is the whole difference, in a single button.

In code, a useful admission. Hexeract, our running thread in Rust, is a broker at heart: its bus builds on RabbitMQ, you publish to a queue, a worker consumes, acknowledges, and the message disappears. Look for an offset or a replay in Hexeract, you will not find one, and that is deliberate: it is proof in code that a broker erases, it does not keep. Wolverine, its dot net counterpart, is instructive because it knows how to speak to both worlds: with a RabbitMQ transport you set the number of competing consumers on a queue, and with a Kafka transport you join a group and can place your offset at the very beginning to re-read everything.

The chapter ends with two pen-and-paper exercises, solved line by line: choosing between broker and log for two needs of our e-commerce order, then reasoning about a fraud-detection service that arrives late and must re-read seven days of payments.

In one sentence. A broker delivers each message to a single consumer then erases it, holding the read position itself; a log keeps the messages and lets each consumer hold its own offset, which allows several independent readers and the replay of history.

Towards chapter three. We now know what becomes of a message, but we glossed over a word heavy with consequences: acknowledgement. Does the mailman acknowledge the letter as he hands it to you, or only after you sign? Lose once, or receive twice: this dilemma is the choice of delivery semantics, and that is the whole subject of chapter three.
