Chapter four. Ordering and idempotency. The previous chapter promised a consumer that recognizes an already-processed message, without saying how. We build it here, and we discover that redelivery does not only bring duplicates: it can also put everything out of order.

Let us start again from chapter three. We saved the payment with a formula: at-least-once, plus idempotent processing. We said the consumer recognizes an already-seen message and does not redo the effect. But recognizes how, with what key, and what stops a crash, landing right between the charge and the already-done note, from making everything start over? And even once that is solved, a second threat is waiting, because at-least-once delivery can replay messages in the wrong order. If order shipped comes back before order paid, no deduplication will save you.

Here is the central idea of the chapter: redelivery inflicts two distinct wounds, and they are often confused. The first is the duplicate, the same message arrives twice and a naive handler charges twice. The second, more insidious, is disorder, because messages replayed or consumed in parallel by several workers no longer respect the causal order. Two wounds, two remedies. Against the duplicate, idempotence. Against disorder, partition ordering. Both rest on the choice of a key, but not the same key.

We first build the idempotent consumer. It needs a way to recognize an already-processed message, that is the deduplication key. Two candidates: the stable message id, set by the producer and kept across redeliveries, or a business key like the order number. The consumer stores these keys in a small table called an inbox. Before acting, it looks: if the key is already there, it has already processed this message, it discards it and acknowledges. Otherwise it processes, then records the key.

And here is the real trap. Imagine a two-step version: first apply the effect, charge forty-nine euros, then record the key in the inbox. If the machine shuts down between the two, the effect happened but the key is not recorded. On redelivery, the consumer does not find the key and charges a second time. We wrote idempotent code that is not. We just reopened the two generals problem inside our own database.

The heart of idempotence is therefore not keeping a list of seen messages, it is guaranteeing that the effect and the recording of the key happen together or not at all. The only clean way is to wrap them in a single database transaction. Either everything is recorded, the effect and the key, or nothing, and the message will be replayed cleanly. It is atomicity that makes idempotence true.

Let us move to the second wound, ordering. The idempotent consumer neutralizes duplicates, but it does not touch ordering. If redelivery makes shipped arrive before paid, a perfectly idempotent consumer will ship an unpaid order, without the slightest alert, because it only checks whether it has already seen this message, never whether it is the right moment.

The solution is to split the stream into partitions, sub-streams each of which keeps its messages in order. The golden rule: ordering is guaranteed only within a partition, never across partitions. This is what we call a partial order, as opposed to a total order that would line up all messages on a single row and kill parallelism. It remains to decide which messages go into which partition, and that is the role of the partition key. All messages that share the same key fall into the same partition, so they stay ordered; different keys spread out and are processed in parallel. For our commerce, the right key is obvious, it is the order id. All events of order forty-two keep their order, and order forty-two and order forty-three move forward in parallel.

In the interactive component, you can take an order delivered at-least-once, out of order and with a duplicate, and turn on or off each of the two ramparts. With nothing, the order is doubly damaged. Deduplication alone, the duplicate disappears but the order stays shipped before payment, that is the central lesson, idempotence does not repair ordering. Ordering alone, the sequence becomes coherent again but the double charge comes back. And only with both ramparts together is the order finally correct.

In code, the lesson is instructive. Wolverine, on a Kafka transport, gives you both ramparts almost for free: a partition key attached to each message for ordering, and the durable inbox that persists before committing the offset and deduplicates by id, that is effectively-once in one call. Hexeract is more raw, and that is what makes it honest: it offers no partitioning by key and warns that dispatch ordering is at best best-effort, and it provides no ready-made inbox, it just propagates a stable message id and documents that handlers must be idempotent. Two philosophies, one same lesson: effectively-once is never magic, it is at-least-once, plus a deduplication, plus a well-chosen partition key.

The chapter ends with two paper-and-pencil exercises, solved line by line: choosing the right partition key between a constant key, the customer id and the order id, and unmasking a fake idempotence when the effect and the key are written in two steps.

In one sentence. At-least-once delivery inflicts two distinct wounds, the duplicate and disorder: we neutralize the duplicate with an idempotent consumer that records its deduplication key atomically with the effect, and we neutralize disorder with a partial order obtained by routing through a partition key the messages that must stay ordered together.

Towards chapter five. We have hardened the consumer, but all this machinery rests on an assumption on the producer's side: it publishes a message when the business state changes. But if it records the order in the database, commits, then fails to publish the message because the network drops it, the database says order created and the broker never received anything. Writing the business state and emitting the message in the same transaction, that is the last lock, and it is the transactional outbox that chapter five builds.
