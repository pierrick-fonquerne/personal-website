Chapter zero. Why talk with messages. When one service calls five others to confirm an order, a single one going down makes everything fail, and we are going to see how the asynchronous message changes that.

Picture this. You click Pay. Behind that click, your order service must notify payment, reserve stock, schedule shipping, send a confirmation email and credit the loyalty program. The question that opens this whole course starts right here: how do these services talk to each other without falling together?

The most natural way is the direct call. Your order service calls payment just as you would make a phone call: you dial the number, and you wait for the other side to pick up and answer before hanging up. As long as everyone picks up quickly, this is perfect. But when the order has to notify all five services one by one, waiting for each reply, two troubles arise. First, time: if each one takes a hundred milliseconds, the user waits for the sum, so half a second, just to see Order accepted. Second, fragility: if the email service is down, the call fails, and the whole order fails with it, even though the email is just a comfort detail. One weak link breaks the entire chain.

Change the image. Instead of phoning, your order writes a note and drops it into a mailbox. It leaves at once, without waiting for anyone to have read it. Each service checks the mailbox when it can. That is what a message is: a piece of information dropped somewhere, that the sender does not wait to see processed. Asynchronous communication is this mode where you drop and carry on, as opposed to the synchronous call where you stay blocked until the reply. The email service is down? Its note waits in the mailbox. The order, meanwhile, is already accepted. The weak link no longer breaks anything: it simply falls behind, on its own, in its corner.

To understand what the message buys us, let us name what it frees us from: coupling. There are three useful forms. Temporal coupling first: both services must be available at the same instant, that is the phone call. Availability coupling next: the health of the caller depends on the health of the callee, if payment goes down, the order goes down. Location coupling last: the caller must know where to reach the callee. The direct synchronous call accumulates all three. The asynchronous message loosens them, because it inserts a queue that accepts the drop even when the recipient is absent.

Beware of one trap: asynchronous does not mean instant. Dropping a message is almost immediate for the sender, but the real work happens later. We have not removed the processing delay, we have moved it off the user's path. The confirmation no longer says everything is done, it says everything is taken into account.

Not all messages are alike, and two very different intentions hide behind the word message. A command message expresses an order addressed to a specific recipient, like Charge the payment. It is named in the imperative, targets a single handler, and the sender expects an effect to happen. An event message, on the other hand, announces a fact that already happened, like Order paid. It is named in the past tense, it is broadcast to whoever wants to listen, and the sender does not know who consumes it. Stock, shipping, email and loyalty can all react to the same event, each on its own side. This switch runs deep: with commands, your order drives, it knows who to call. With events, it just announces I am paid and loses interest in the rest. That is what lets you add a sixth service tomorrow without touching the order service.

In the chapter, a component lets you play with all of this. You place an order that must notify five services, you choose synchronous or asynchronous mode, you take a service down, and you watch two things: how long the caller stays blocked, and what happens to each service. Take the first service down in synchronous mode, and you will see the following ones stay not reached: the chain stops at the first broken link.

This distinction between command and event is not just a mental view, frameworks make it explicit right down to their method names. With Hexeract, a messaging framework in Rust, a command is sent with send and awaits its result, while an event is broadcast with publish, without waiting for anything. With Wolverine, its dot net counterpart, it is the same: InvokeAsync for the command that awaits a reply, PublishAsync for the event that gets broadcast. The pattern is the same on both sides: a verb that waits for the command, a verb that broadcasts for the event. Remember the pattern, not the syntax, because it is the message's intention that chooses the method, never the other way around.

The chapter ends with two pen-and-paper exercises solved step by step: classify four messages as command or event, then compute the user's waiting time. In synchronous mode, fifty plus thirty plus twenty plus ten plus fifteen make one hundred twenty-five milliseconds, and this total grows with every service. In asynchronous mode, the caller only pays the cost of the drop, about five milliseconds, whatever the number of services.

In one sentence. Talking with messages means replacing a direct call, which couples two services in time and in failure, with an asynchronous drop that decouples them: the sender carries on with its work, and we distinguish the command, which orders and waits, from the event, which states and broadcasts.

Towards the next chapter. We contrasted the command that orders and the event that states, but one common case is missing: when a service only needs to ask for information, without changing anything. What is this customer's loyalty balance is neither an order nor a past fact. It is a third family, the query, and it is through it that the next chapter will complete the taxonomy of messages before we tackle delivery guarantees.
