Chapter three. Delivery semantics. Everything turns on a single instant: the one when we acknowledge a message. Too early, we risk losing it; too late, we risk processing it twice.

In chapter two, we saw what becomes of a message: erased after acknowledgement on the broker, kept and re-readable on the log. We even slipped in a loaded phrase: acknowledged equals erased. But we left a gap. Go back to the postal carrier. At what exact instant does he write down delivered? The moment he hands you the envelope, or only after you have signed? Between those two instants hides the possibility of an accident, and depending on the choice, your message is either lost or received twice.

Let us start with that instant. When the carrier writes delivered in his notebook and throws away his copy, that gesture is the acknowledgement: the signal that says, this is settled, it can be erased. Once the copy is gone, no one will replay that letter. The whole question is one word: when? First choice, he writes delivered the moment he hands you the envelope, before you have even taken it. If he throws away his copy and you drop the letter in a puddle right after, it is lost: you received it at most once, maybe zero times. Second choice, he waits for your signature. But if lightning strikes just after you sign, and before he records your signature, he will come back tomorrow to deliver the same letter: you will have received it at least once, sometimes twice. Lose once, or receive twice. That is the heart of delivery semantics.

Let us give them their names. For a message, a system always runs three actions: it delivers the message, the consumer processes it, that is the real effect like charging a card, and someone acknowledges. What changes everything is the order of these actions. The first discipline acknowledges before processing: it erases early, and a crash before the end of processing loses the message. This is at-most-once delivery: zero or one time, never more, we never duplicate but we can lose. The second acknowledges after successful processing, and redelivers as long as no acknowledgement arrives: a crash after processing but before the acknowledgement triggers a redelivery, hence a duplicate. This is at-least-once delivery: one time or more, never zero, we never lose but we can duplicate. The third is the dream: exactly once, neither loss nor duplicate. And that is where it breaks.

Why is exactly-once delivery impossible? Because of an old riddle, the two generals problem. Two allied armies camp on two hills, separated by a valley held by the enemy. To win, they must attack at the same moment. They communicate only through messengers who can be captured. The first general sends: attack at dawn. He needs an acknowledgement. The second sends back: agreed. But this second messenger can be captured too, so the second general does not know whether his acknowledgement arrived, and he will not dare attack. We would need an acknowledgement of the acknowledgement, then yet another, forever. It can be proved that no finite protocol makes both generals jointly certain of the same plan. The link to our messages is direct: the sender can never be sure the message was processed exactly once, because the acknowledgement itself can be lost. It therefore has only two behaviors: do not retry and risk loss, or retry and risk a duplicate. No third way. Exactly-once delivery is a myth.

So we move the problem. We accept duplicates at delivery, but we make them harmless at processing. Processing the same message twice must produce the same result as processing it once. This property is called idempotence. An image: pressing the call floor three button a second time, when it is already requested, does not request it twice. An idempotent processing is the same: the second pass of an already-seen message does not redo the effect, it recognizes it and skips. The combination, at-least-once delivery plus idempotent processing, has a name: effectively-once delivery. We do not remove the duplicates, we neutralize them. From the point of view of the effect, the payment is charged only once. How do we concretely build that idempotent processing? We name it here, we will build it in the next chapter. And be careful: when you read that Kafka does exactly-once, it is true but bounded to its own world. As soon as an effect leaves Kafka, charging a real card for instance, you fall back on the general rule: at-least-once, plus idempotence.

In the interactive component, you can run the same message, charging a payment, through all three disciplines at once, and choose the instant of the crash. With no crash, all three charge once. Place the crash after delivery and before processing: at-most-once loses the payment, zero charges, while the other two redeliver and end up at one. Finally place the crash after processing and before acknowledgement, the truly revealing moment: at-least-once charges twice, the duplicate, while effectively-once recognizes the already-seen message and stays at one. The whole lesson of the chapter, in one selector.

In code, these disciplines are not theories, they are dials. Hexeract, our common thread in Rust, exposes an acknowledgement mode set on the worker, with three variants. The manual mode, which is the default, acknowledges after successful processing: it is at-least-once, and the code comment states verbatim that handlers must be idempotent, because duplicates can occur. That is this chapter proved by code. The acknowledge-on-receipt mode is at-most-once. And a third mode, with no acknowledgement at all, pushes the logic to the end: all or nothing, with no safety net. Wolverine, its .NET counterpart, provides the idempotence safety net out of the box: on a Kafka transport, you ask for a durable inbox, which persists each message before committing the read position, and which automatically discards an already-processed identifier. Effectively-once delivery becomes a single line. Two ecosystems, one same lesson: you explicitly choose your semantic, and effectively-once is never magic, it is at-least-once plus deduplication.

The chapter ends with two paper-and-pencil exercises, solved line by line: choosing the right semantic for a click stream then for charging a payment, and reasoning about an accident that falls just after processing, before the acknowledgement, across the three disciplines.

In one sentence. The instant of the acknowledgement relative to processing decides the guarantee: acknowledging before gives at-most-once, you can lose; acknowledging after gives at-least-once, you can duplicate; exactly-once delivery is impossible because of the two generals, and we imitate it with at-least-once delivery paired with idempotent processing.

Towards chapter four. We know how to choose our delivery promise, and that the only safe way goes through idempotence. But we invoked it without saying how we build it: how does a worker recognize an already-processed message, with what key, and without reopening the two generals problem inside its own database? And at-least-once delivery redelivers, but in what order? Chapter four, ordering and idempotence, builds the idempotent consumer and handles ordering by key and by partition.
