In chapters 1 and 2 you met the <Term slug="fonction-activation">activation function</Term> $f$ in the formula $y = f(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} + b)$ without really spending time on it. That's the subject of this chapter. We will see why it is mathematically essential, compare the four classics (Identity, sigmoid, ReLU, tanh) and learn how to choose between them.

<Callout kind="info" title="Prerequisites and level">
**What you need to know**: chapter 1 (neuron), the notation $f(z)$ of a real function of one variable, ideally the notion of a derivative (revisit chapter 7 if needed; it is only required in the last section).

**What is not required**: advanced differential calculus, approximation theory, numerical optimisation.

**Level of rigour**: each function gets an equation, a derivative and its limits. The central proof (without non-linearity, depth is pointless) is explicit.
</Callout>

## Why we need a non-linear function

### The proof that justifies everything

Consider a two-layer network with no activation function, i.e. $f$ replaced by the identity. The first layer outputs:

$$
\mathbf{h} = W_1 \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1
$$

The second layer applied to $\mathbf{h}$ gives:

$$
\mathbf{y} = W_2 \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{b}_2 = W_2 (W_1 \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1) + \mathbf{b}_2 = (W_2 W_1) \mathbf{x} + (W_2 \mathbf{b}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2)
$$

Setting $W' = W_2 W_1$ and $\mathbf{b}' = W_2 \mathbf{b}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2$, we recover:

$$
\mathbf{y} = W' \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}'
$$

**That is a single affine layer.** Stacking layers without non-linearity is mathematically pointless: the whole thing reduces to one equivalent layer. This is the **reason for being** of the activation function.

<Callout kind="tip" title="The theorem in one sentence">
*Without non-linearity, network depth is an illusion.* That is why we insert a function $f$ at the output of every neuron, to "break" linearity. Without it, you do not have a deep network, you have a linear regression with fancy notation.
</Callout>

### What we expect from an activation function

To be usable in practice, a function $f$ has to tick several boxes:

1. **Non-linear** (for the reason we just saw).
2. **Differentiable** almost everywhere, so <Term slug="backpropagation">backpropagation</Term> can compute a gradient (chapter 8).
3. **Fast to compute**, because we evaluate it millions of times per second during training.
4. **Bounded** (ideally), to keep outputs from blowing up.
5. **Non-zero gradient** over a wide range, otherwise training stalls.

The four classics we are about to study tick those boxes to varying degrees.

## The four classical functions

### Identity

The identity is the function that does nothing. We include it for completeness and because we use it on the **output layer** for regression problems.

<Formula caption="Identity">
f(x) = x
</Formula>

**Equation**: $f(x) = x$. **Derivative**: $f'(x) = 1$.

You can safely ignore this function as long as we are inside hidden layers, but it returns in chapter 6 when we discuss loss functions for regression.

### Sigmoid

The <Term slug="sigmoide">sigmoid</Term> dominated deep learning between 1986 and 2010. Its S-shape squashes any real number into the open interval $]0, 1[$, which lets us read its output as a probability.

<Formula caption="Sigmoid">
σ(x) = 1 / (1 + e⁻ˣ)
</Formula>

**Equation**: $\sigma(x) = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$. **Derivative**: $\sigma'(x) = \sigma(x)(1 - \sigma(x))$, an elegant property that simplifies backpropagation.

**Shape**: S-curve, $\sigma(0) = 0.5$, $\sigma(-\infty) \to 0$, $\sigma(+\infty) \to 1$.

**Modern usage**: output layer for binary classification (where the probability has a meaning). Rarely used in deep hidden layers because of the vanishing gradient (next section).

### ReLU

<Term slug="relu">ReLU</Term> is today the default choice for hidden layers. Its brutal simplicity is precisely what makes it efficient.

<Formula caption="ReLU">
ReLU(x) = max(0, x)
</Formula>

**Equation**: $\text{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x)$. **Derivative**: $\text{ReLU}'(x) = \mathbb{1}[x > 0]$, i.e. $1$ for $x > 0$ and $0$ otherwise. Strictly undefined at $x = 0$, but we conventionally set it to $0$ or $1$ with no practical consequence.

**Shape**: zero on negatives, identity on positives. Not bounded above.

**Modern usage**: 90 % of modern deep networks use ReLU or one of its variants (Leaky ReLU, ELU, GELU) in their hidden layers (Nair and Hinton, 2010). It computes in one operation, its gradient is exact (1 or 0), and it does not saturate upwards. Drawback: if a neuron's input stays negative, its gradient is zero and it stops learning. This is the *dying ReLU* problem, mitigated by the variants.

### Tanh

The hyperbolic tangent is the centred cousin of the sigmoid. Same S-shape, but squashes into $]-1, 1[$ instead of $]0, 1[$.

<Formula caption="Tanh">
tanh(x) = (eˣ - e⁻ˣ) / (eˣ + e⁻ˣ)
</Formula>

**Equation**: $\tanh(x) = \dfrac{e^x - e^{-x}}{e^x + e^{-x}}$. **Derivative**: $\tanh'(x) = 1 - \tanh^2(x)$.

**Shape**: S-curve, $\tanh(0) = 0$, $\tanh(-\infty) \to -1$, $\tanh(+\infty) \to +1$.

**Modern usage**: favoured when you want a zero-centred output (statistically preferable for training). Often used in classical RNNs (LSTM, GRU) and in some attention layers.

- Watch the ReLU derivative: a step to zero for negatives. No gradient flows back, so no learning on that branch.

## The vanishing gradient problem

When we differentiate the sigmoid, we get $\sigma'(x) = \sigma(x)(1 - \sigma(x))$. The maximum is reached at $x = 0$ and equals $0.25$. So at every layer crossed, the gradient is multiplied by a factor of at most $0.25$.

For a 10-layer network using sigmoid, the gradient at the first layer is multiplied by at most $0.25^{10} \approx 9.5 \times 10^{-7}$. That is extremely small: the first layer no longer learns. This is the **vanishing gradient problem**, identified rigorously by Glorot and Bengio in 2010.

ReLU largely solves this problem: on its active part, the gradient is exactly $1$. Multiplying by $1$ does not shrink the gradient. This is one of the two reasons (with its computational speed) for its modern dominance.

<Callout kind="warn" title="The vanishing-gradient trap">
If you train a deep network and the first layer stays mute while the last one converges, you almost certainly have a vanishing gradient. First reflexes: swap sigmoids for ReLUs, check the initialisation (chapter 11), and try batch normalisation (same chapter).
</Callout>

## How to choose in practice

A simple heuristic that works 95 % of the time:

| Layer | Default choice | Variants |
|---|---|---|
| Hidden layers | **ReLU** | Leaky ReLU, ELU, GELU for dying-ReLU cases |
| Binary classification output | **Sigmoid** | (none) |
| Multi-class classification output | **Softmax** (chapter 6) | (none) |
| Regression output | **Identity** | (none) |
| Recurrent layers (RNN, LSTM, GRU) | **Tanh** + sigmoid on gates | (none) |

This table is a heuristic, not dogma. Specific architectures (transformers, GANs, diffusion models) use other functions (GELU, Swish, Mish). But for a standard network, ReLU everywhere except output is an excellent starting point.

## In one sentence

The activation function is what stops a deep network from collapsing into a linear regression. Sigmoid and tanh ruled until 2010, ReLU has dominated since. The choice depends on context but rarely turns out to be tricky in practice.

## Exercises

### Exercise 1: differentiate the sigmoid

Starting from the definition $\sigma(x) = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$, prove that $\sigma'(x) = \sigma(x)(1 - \sigma(x))$.

### Exercise 2: compute a derivative at a specific value

Compute $\sigma'(0)$, then $\sigma'(2)$. Compare those two values. What does this tell you about the behaviour of the gradient for inputs far from zero?

### Exercise 3: compare the speed

Consider a neuron taking 1000 inputs. How many elementary arithmetic operations (additions, multiplications, exponentials) are needed to compute the output of a neuron with ReLU activation? With sigmoid activation? Does the difference look marginal or significant when multiplied by millions of evaluations?

<Callout kind="tip" title="Solutions">
**Exercise 1.** We differentiate using the chain rule. Let $u(x) = 1 + e^{-x}$, so $\sigma(x) = 1/u(x)$ and $\sigma'(x) = -u'(x)/u(x)^2$. With $u'(x) = -e^{-x}$, we get $\sigma'(x) = \dfrac{e^{-x}}{(1 + e^{-x})^2}$.

We recognise $\dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} = \sigma(x)$ and $\dfrac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}} = 1 - \sigma(x)$ (check by adding those two fractions, you get 1). Hence $\sigma'(x) = \sigma(x)(1 - \sigma(x))$. ∎

**Exercise 2.** $\sigma(0) = 1/2$, so $\sigma'(0) = (1/2)(1/2) = 1/4 = 0.25$. This is the maximum of the derivative.

$\sigma(2) \approx 0.881$, so $\sigma'(2) \approx 0.881 \times 0.119 \approx 0.105$. The gradient is divided by about $2.4$ between 0 and 2. For larger inputs the gradient collapses very quickly: this is the **saturation** of the sigmoid and the source of the vanishing-gradient problem.

**Exercise 3.** For ReLU: 1000 multiplications + 999 additions + 1 comparison with 0 = around 2000 operations.

For sigmoid: same for the weighted sum, plus one exponential + 1 addition + 1 division = around 2003 operations in raw count. But the exponential actually costs in the order of 20 to 50 CPU cycles on commodity hardware, against 1 cycle for a comparison. With billions of evaluations per second in a deep network, this 20-50× factor on the activation becomes a 5 to 10× factor on overall throughput. It is significant, and it is the second reason for ReLU's dominance.
</Callout>
