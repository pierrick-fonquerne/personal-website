Chapter seven. Derivatives and the chain rule. From the slope of a curve to the gradient, the tool that turns the cost landscape into a path of descent.

In chapter six, we learned to compute a network's score and read it as an altitude in a cost landscape. Good networks sit in the valleys, bad ones on the peaks, and learning means descending toward a low point. The decisive question remains. Around the point where we currently stand, in which direction should each weight be adjusted to bring the score down? To answer it, we need to measure which way the surface tilts. That local tilt has a precise mathematical name, the derivative. This chapter lays down three tools, and each one has a clear geometric reading. The derivative measures the slope of a curve at a point. The chain rule propagates that slope through nested computations. The gradient generalises the slope to many weights at once.

First act, the derivative is a slope. Take the curve of x squared, and pick a point on it. If you zoom in very close, the curve eventually looks like a straight line. The slope of that line is the derivative at that point. To define it, we start from the rate of change between two nearby points, x and x plus h. That is the slope of the secant, the line cutting the curve at those two points. As we let the gap h tend to zero, the second point slides toward the first, and the secant tips over toward the tangent. The derivative is the limit of that rate. For x squared, it equals two x. The slope is therefore two at x equals one, it is zero at the bottom of the bowl, and minus two to the left. Also remember the sigmoid's derivative, remarkable because its slope is expressed in terms of its own value, a times one minus a. In the component, slide the point and narrow the gap h to watch the secant's slope join the exact slope.

Second act, the chain rule. A network is not a single function but a stack of nested functions. The output of one layer feeds the next. To differentiate such a stack, we need to differentiate a composition of functions, of the form f of g of x. The chain rule says the derivative of a composition is the product of the local derivatives. We multiply the slopes along the path. Take an example. If u equals three x plus one, and y equals u squared, then the derivative of y with respect to x equals two u times three, which is six times three x plus one. At x equals one, that gives twenty-four.

And here is the key point of the chapter. The same mechanism is a neuron. Take a single neuron and its score. The input passes through three nested computations. First the pre-activation, z equals w x plus b. Then the activation, a equals sigmoid of z. Finally the score, L equals a minus y squared. To know how to adjust the weight w, we need the derivative of L with respect to w, and we multiply three local derivatives along the path. Two times a minus y, times a times one minus a, times x. You have just computed how the score responds to a weight, by propagating derivatives backward through the graph. That is exactly backpropagation. Chapter eight will simply apply this mechanically to a full network.

Third act, partial derivatives and the gradient. A real network has millions of weights. The score is then a function of all those weights at once. How do we talk about slope when there are many directions to move in? We look at one direction at a time. The partial derivative with respect to one weight is the slope obtained by varying only that weight, all others held constant. Stack all those slopes into a vector, and you get the gradient. The gradient is not just a list of numbers. Seen as an arrow, it points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its length measures the steepness. To bring the score down, we go in the opposite direction, minus the gradient. We finally hold the compass of descent. In the component, move the point on the landscape and watch the gradient arrow, and its opposite pointing toward the valley.

The chapter ends with four pen-and-paper exercises, solved line by line. A derivative from the definition, a chain rule, the gradient of a neuron, and a gradient with its direction of descent.

In one sentence. The derivative measures the slope of a function at a point, the chain rule propagates that slope through nested computations by multiplying the local derivatives, and the gradient stacks those slopes into a compass that tells, for each weight, which way to bring the score down.

Toward chapter eight. We now know how to compute the slope of the score with respect to a single isolated weight. But a real network has thousands of weights chained across many layers, and redoing this computation from scratch for each one would be ruinously expensive. Backpropagation, in chapter eight, organises this computation into a single backward pass through the network that recovers all the partial derivatives at once. It is the chain rule, industrialised.
