Chapter ten. Regularisation and generalisation. How to stop a network from memorising, and force it to generalise.

In chapter nine, we proved that gradient descent drives the score down, step after step, as long as the step stays reasonable. That is a real guarantee. But it concerns the training score, the one computed on the examples the network has in front of it. And a polynomial of degree nine can pass exactly through ten measurement points, with a score of zero, while oscillating wildly between them. So the question of this chapter is this. How can we tell whether a network has learned the trend, or only the points? And above all, how do we stop it from memorising without stopping it from learning? We will see that the classical answers can be proven, and that they are closer to one another than they look.

Act one, memorising is not learning. Picture a student preparing for an exam with the past papers of the last ten years. They retain every model answer, word for word, and score full marks on the past papers. On exam day, the paper is new, and they are stuck. They learned the answers, not the method. A network runs exactly the same risk. What we ask of it is to answer well on examples it has never seen, and that is generalisation. But what we make it minimise is the score on its training examples. When that score is excellent while the performance on new examples is poor, we speak of overfitting.

The polynomial that goes everywhere is the textbook example. With ten points whose abscissas are all different, there always exists a polynomial of degree at most nine that passes through all of them: the Lagrange interpolation polynomial. For each point, we build a product that equals one at that point and zero at all the others, then we add them up. The training score is exactly zero. But if the measurements are noisy, this polynomial also hugs the noise, and makes loops between the points that have nothing to do with the trend. Runge even showed, in nineteen oh one, that interpolation through equally spaced points can oscillate more and more strongly as the degree grows. A network does the same. The universal approximation theorem of chapter five says that a wide enough network can represent almost any function, and therefore any memorisation too. In 2017, Zhang and his co-authors replaced the labels of an image dataset with labels drawn at random. The networks still reached a perfect training score, and answered at random on new images.

A boxed section gives the exact reason behind this: the bias-variance decomposition. The average error of a model on a new measurement splits into three non-negative pieces. The noise, which nobody can remove. The squared bias, the gap between the average prediction and the truth, large when the model is too rigid. And the variance, which measures how much the prediction changes from one training set to another, large when the model is too flexible. The proof cuts the gap into three, squares it, and checks that every cross term has zero mean. Regularising means accepting a little bias to gain a lot of variance.

Act two, three datasets. Since the training score lies, we set aside examples that the network will never see during training. The training set is used to compute gradients. The validation set is used to make choices, such as the degree, the regularisation strength, or when to stop. And the test set serves a single purpose: announcing the final performance, once every choice has been made. We do not merge validation and test, because each choice is a small dose of learning, and a score we have made choices on becomes optimistic. Comparing the two scores gives a diagnosis. Both poor means underfitting. Very good in training and poor in validation means overfitting. Both good and close means a good balance. Everything we add to training to improve generalisation shares a common name: regularisation.

Act three, the L2 penalty, also called weight decay. The oscillating polynomial has huge coefficients, which cancel each other out to pass through every point. So the idea is to penalise large weights. We minimise the score, plus lambda times the sum of the squared weights. The gradient of this fine is two lambda times w, and a descent step becomes: the new weight equals one minus two eta lambda, times the old weight, minus eta times the gradient of the score. Even before the correction, each step therefore multiplies every weight by a number slightly below one. If the data do not call for a weight, it melts away on its own, hence the name. In practice, only the weights are penalised, not the biases.

To find out exactly what this penalty does, we study a sum of parabolas, one per weight, each with its own curvature a i and its own minimum w i star. This is the shape any smooth score takes near a minimum, after a good change of axes. The shrinkage theorem then says that the penalised minimum is a i over a i plus lambda, times w i star. The proof is short: the penalised score splits into independent terms, we differentiate each one, set the derivative to zero, and check that the parabola opens upwards. The reading is illuminating. If the curvature is large compared with lambda, the factor is close to one, and the weight is almost untouched. If it is small, the factor is close to zero, and the weight is crushed. Now, a low curvature means the data hardly know what to do with that weight, and that is exactly where noise settles. The L2 penalty keeps what the data determine clearly, and erases the rest. This is the ridge regression of Hoerl and Kennard, in nineteen seventy. A bonus for descent: the curvature becomes a i plus lambda, and the ravine of chapter nine softens.

In the lab, there are ten noisy points from a sine wave. At degree nine with no penalty, the curve passes exactly through the ten points, but the validation error is poor. As you raise lambda, the training error always increases, while the validation error drops and then rises again, drawing a U whose bottom is the right setting. With a huge lambda, the curve flattens towards the mean: that is underfitting.

Act four, the L1 penalty and sparsity. This time, the fine uses the absolute value of the weights instead of their square. It looks like a detail, but it is a difference in kind: L1 sets weights exactly to zero, which L2 never does. The chapter proves it on a single weight, by minimising w minus c, squared, plus lambda times the absolute value of w. We reason by cases. If c is greater than lambda over two, the minimum is c minus lambda over two. If c is less than minus lambda over two, it is c plus lambda over two. And in between, the minimum is exactly zero. This is soft thresholding: each weight is moved towards zero by a fixed amount, and if it cannot afford it, it switches off. With L2, in the same situation, we get c over one plus lambda, never zero. L2 trims every weight, L1 switches some of them off. A model in which many weights are zero is called sparse: this is the lasso of Tibshirani.

Act five, early stopping. This method does not touch the score, it touches the schedule. We measure the validation score at every epoch. At first it goes down, then it starts climbing while the training score keeps falling: the network is starting to learn the noise. We keep the weights from the best moment, with a little patience so as not to stop on a mere hiccup. It looks like an engineering trick, but it is a true regularisation. On the same sum of parabolas, starting from zero weights, the trajectory theorem says that after t steps, each weight equals one minus, one minus two eta a i, to the power t, all times w i star. This is exactly the geometric sequence of chapter nine, applied to the gap from the minimum. Steep directions converge in a few steps, flat directions barely move. Stopping early therefore keeps the well-determined directions, just like L2. A corollary makes this precise, using Bernoulli's inequality, proved by induction: both factors, the one from early stopping and the one from L2, stay under the same line when we choose lambda equal to one over two eta t. The number of steps plays the role of the inverse of the penalty. Be careful, the corollary does not say the two methods are identical: the equivalence is an approximation, a very good one in practice.

In the lab, in early stopping mode, the curve starts completely flat, first takes the shape of the sine wave, then twists to catch every point. The validation error reaches its minimum fairly early, then rises again. And that best point is very close to the one for the best lambda in penalty mode: two roads to the same regularisation.

Act six, dropout. During training, at each step, we randomly switch off some of the hidden neurons. Each one is kept with probability p, and we draw again at every step. Think of a football team that trains without knowing which players will show up: nobody can rely on one specific teammate, so everyone learns to be useful on their own. In the so-called inverted version, the output of a kept neuron is divided by p. And the chapter proves that, thanks to this division, the output keeps exactly the same mean over all the draws. Consequence: at prediction time, we turn dropout off and use the full network, with no correction at all. Each mask defines a sub-network, and with a hundred neurons there are more than ten to the power thirty of them, all sharing the same weights. Predicting with the full network means letting this whole crowd vote, and an average of many models has less variance than each of them.

The chapter ends with five pencil-and-paper exercises, solved line by line. A diagnosis of underfitting and overfitting, a descent step with weight decay, shrinkage according to curvature, a comparison of L1 and L2 on three weights, and a numerical comparison between early stopping and the L2 penalty.

In one sentence. A network can learn its examples by heart, and regularisation, whether it is the L2 penalty that shrinks blurry directions, the L1 penalty that switches weights off, early stopping that acts like an L2 penalty, or dropout that lets a crowd of sub-networks vote, trades a little training score for much better generalisation, tuned on a validation set.

Towards chapter eleven. Early stopping starts from zero weights, and the L2 penalty pulls the weights towards zero. So why not start a network with all its weights at zero? Because it would be fatal. Go back to the toy network of chapter eight and its two hidden neurons. If they have the same weights at the start, they compute the same output, receive the same error signal, and undergo the same update, forever. Two twin neurons are worth only one. Conversely, weights that are too large saturate the sigmoids, and we fall back on the vanishing gradient. We therefore need to start from weights that are small, but different, and at the right scale. Chapter eleven computes that scale, then shows how to maintain it throughout training: this is initialisation, and batch normalisation.
