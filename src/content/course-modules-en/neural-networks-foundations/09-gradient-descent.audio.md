Chapter nine. Gradient descent. How to use the gradient to drive the score down, iteration after iteration, and why the length of the step decides everything.

In chapter eight, backpropagation handed us, in a single backward pass, the slope of the score with respect to every weight of the network. We hold the complete compass. But a compass has never moved anyone forward, and knowing where to go down is not going down. So the question of this chapter is very concrete. How do we turn this gradient into a step, and how long should that step be? Too short, and we never arrive. Too long, and we jump over the valley, and may even end up higher than where we started. We will see that this intuition can be proved, and that it yields a precise rule.

First act, the hiker in the fog. Picture yourself in the mountains, caught in thick fog. You want to reach the valley, but the only information within reach is the slope of the ground under your feet. So you feel which way the ground drops most steeply, you take one step that way, and you start again. That is exactly gradient descent. The gradient points towards the steepest rise of the score, so we step in the opposite direction. The rule reads like this: the new weight equals the old one, minus eta times the gradient of the score. The small number eta is the learning rate, and it sets the length of the step. One descent step is therefore a forward pass, a backward pass, then this subtraction on every weight. Repeat, and that is all. You have already met a rule of this shape with the perceptron, in chapter four. But the perceptron corrected by projection, with no slope, whereas here every correction is dictated by the derivative of the score. And the idea is old: Augustin Cauchy proposed it as early as 1847, for orbit computations.

Second act, getting eta right, proved on a parabola. Take the simplest score that has a hollow, a times w squared, with a positive. The number a measures the curvature, and the larger it is, the narrower the bowl. Its derivative is two a times w. Applying the rule, the new weight equals one minus two eta a, times the old weight. This line says it all, because at every step the weight is multiplied by the same number. That is a geometric sequence, which tends to zero if and only if this factor lies strictly between minus one and one, that is, if eta lies between zero and one over a. Five exact regimes follow. Below one over two a, the weight slides towards the bottom without changing sign. At exactly one over two a, the first step lands right at the bottom. Between one over two a and one over a, the weight jumps from one side to the other, but each jump is shorter, so it oscillates and still converges. At exactly one over a, it bounces forever. And beyond that, each jump is longer than the last, and the descent diverges. Keep the deep lesson in mind: the threshold depends on the curvature, so a narrow bowl demands a small step. In the lab, set eta and take steps. Start around zero point one, then cross zero point five, then one, and watch the trajectory go from a calm slide, to a zigzag, to an explosion.

Third act, the descent lemma. A real network does not have such a simple score. Can we still guarantee that a step lowers the score? Yes, provided the slope does not change too abruptly. If the hiker knows the terrain has no cliff and no hidden ledge, then the slope under their feet stays reliable over some distance, and they can take a step with confidence. We make this idea precise. A function is called beta smooth if the gap between two of its slopes never exceeds beta times the gap between the two points. For the parabola, beta equals two a. The descent lemma then states that a beta smooth function always stays below a parabola that touches its graph at the current point, with the same slope. The proof is short. We study the gap between this ceiling and the function. It is zero at the starting point, and thanks to smoothness its derivative is negative on the left and positive on the right. So the gap decreases then increases, it reaches its minimum at the starting point, and that minimum is zero. The gap is never negative. Applied to a descent step, this lemma gives the central guarantee of the chapter: if eta does not exceed one over beta, every step lowers the score by at least eta over two, times the square of the slope. On the parabola, we recover exactly the two thresholds found by hand. The result carries over unchanged to millions of weights, by looking at the score along the single line of the step, and it is the lemma found in Nesterov, at the heart of nearly every analysis of descent. But be careful about what it promises. Adding up the guarantees step after step, we show that the slopes we meet become as small as we like. The descent therefore stops at a point where the slope is zero, and a zero slope is not necessarily the bottom of the valley.

Fourth act, batch, mini-batch and stochastic. The score is really an average over all the training examples, and so is its gradient. With sixty thousand images, a single step would demand sixty thousand backpropagations. That is full batch, exact but terribly slow. The idea that unlocks everything is not to wait until we have seen everything. We randomly draw a small batch of examples, a mini-batch, and estimate the gradient on that batch alone. The estimate is noisy, but it is right on average: if we draw one example at random, the average of its gradient over all possible draws is exactly the true gradient. We call this an unbiased estimator. With a single example at a time, we speak of stochastic gradient descent, an idea that goes back to Robbins and Monro, in 1951. A full pass over all the data is called an epoch. With sixty thousand examples and batches of one hundred, an epoch counts six hundred steps, where full batch takes only one. And the noise even has an advantage: it shakes the trajectory, which can help it leave a flat spot or a pass.

Fifth act, the traps of the terrain. Here are the places where the slope vanishes without us being at the bottom. First, local minima, hollows lower than their neighbours but higher than the true bottom. Convex functions, shaped like a bowl with no bump, have none: for them, any point of zero slope is the global minimum. Unfortunately, the score of a network with a hidden layer is not convex in general. Next, saddle points, mountain passes, a minimum in some directions and a maximum in others. Dauphin and his coauthors argue that in very high dimension, these are what we mostly meet, and that the real danger is slowing down for a long time around them. Then plateaus, where the slope is tiny and so is the step, cousins of the vanishing gradient from chapter eight. Finally, ravines. Take a score ten times steeper in one direction than in the other. Each direction has its own threshold, and with a single eta for everyone, we are stuck: large, we zigzag from one wall to the other, small, we crawl along the floor. Switch the lab to ravine mode to see this zigzag, and raise eta above zero point one to watch the steep direction explode, while the flat direction could have handled a step ten times larger.

The chapter ends with five pen-and-paper exercises, solved line by line. One descent step on the toy network from chapter eight, three learning rates on a parabola, the thresholds of a steeper bowl, the descent lemma against the exact computation, and counting the steps in an epoch.

In one sentence. Gradient descent takes a step opposite to the gradient, of length eta, and the descent lemma guarantees that the score goes down as long as that step stays below one over beta, where beta measures how fast the slope can change.

Towards chapter ten. We now know how to lower the score, with a proven guarantee. But it is the score on the training examples, and only that one. Think of a curve that must pass through ten measured points. A polynomial of degree nine can go through all of them exactly, and swing wildly in between. It has learned the points by heart, not the trend. A large enough network can do the same, and gradient descent will help it with all its might. Chapter ten teaches how to descend without learning by heart: that is regularisation.
