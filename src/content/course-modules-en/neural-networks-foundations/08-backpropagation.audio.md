Chapter eight. Backpropagation. A single backward pass that recovers the gradient of every weight, the chain rule, industrialised.

In chapter seven, we learned to compute the derivative of the score with respect to a single weight, by multiplying local derivatives along the graph of one neuron. That was the chain rule, laid down brick by brick. But a real network does not have one weight, it has thousands, chained across several layers. Recomputing that from scratch for every weight would be ruinous. So the question of this chapter is precise. How do we get the derivative of the score with respect to every weight at once, in a single pass, without repeating the same multiplications a thousand times? The answer is an algorithm of rare elegance, and you already hold all its pieces. All that remains is to organise them.

First act, the cost of brute force. Recall the end of chapter seven. For a single neuron, the derivative of the score with respect to the weight was a product of three factors, along the path from the weight to the score. Simple. Now stack two layers. The score depends on the weights of the last layer, but also on those of the hidden layer, which act earlier and whose effect must cross the whole rest of the network. The naive approach treats each weight separately, and starts again from the score for each one. The problem is obvious. Two weights that end at the same output neuron share the same start of the chain. Recomputing it for each is redoing the same multiplication over and over. On a million weights, that is a million almost identical chains, recomputed for nothing. The liberating idea fits in one sentence. What if we computed the shared pieces once, stored them, and reused them? That is precisely what backpropagation does.

Second act, the error signal and the two passes. The piece shared by every chain passing through a neuron is the sensitivity of the score to that neuron. We give it a name, the error signal, written delta. It is the derivative of the score with respect to the neuron's pre-activation. It reads, how much the score would change if the net input of this neuron shifted by a hair. And here is the master formula of the chapter. A weight gradient is the product of the error signal of the arriving neuron, by the activation of the departing neuron. So all the work reduces to computing the deltas, and for that we proceed in two passes. The forward pass stores. We run a normal forward pass, from inputs to output, but this time we keep in memory every pre-activation and every activation. The backward pass propagates the error. It starts from the output and climbs back. At the output neuron, the error signal equals two times a minus y, times a times one minus a. Then we step back one layer. The error signal of a hidden neuron is the error of the neurons it feeds, brought back to it by weighting with the connection weights, then multiplied by its own local slope, sigma prime of z. That is the whole meaning of the prefix back. The error propagates backwards, from output to input. In the component, you step through it. The forward pass lights up the network from left to right, then the backward pass carries the error signal back from right to left, and each weight gradient appears as the product of two already computed numbers.

Third act, why backward, and the product that collapses. The direction of the backward pass is not arbitrary, it is forced by the computation graph. The arrows of computation go from input to output. The derivatives travel the opposite way, because to know how the score depends on a deep weight, we must climb the whole chain. The computation descends, the error climbs. And this climb has a heavy consequence. The error signal of a deep neuron is a product. At each layer crossed backwards, we multiply by a new factor, sigma prime of z. But the derivative of the sigmoid never exceeds a quarter. Multiplying numbers all below a quarter, layer after layer, melts the result exponentially. After a few layers, the first layers barely learn any more. This is the vanishing gradient. In the simulator, stack layers and watch the gradient collapse. Toggle the activation, and compare. The sigmoid crushes everything, whereas ReLU, whose derivative equals one in its active region, lets the signal through. This is one of the main reasons for the massive shift to ReLU.

The chapter ends with four pencil-and-paper exercises, solved line by line. A forward pass by hand, an output error signal, a backpropagation to a hidden layer, and the product of derivatives that collapses with depth.

In one sentence. Backpropagation computes the gradient of every weight in one forward pass that stores the activations and one backward pass that propagates the error signal from output to input, each weight gradient being the product of the arriving neuron's error signal by the departing neuron's activation.

Towards chapter nine. We can now compute, in a single backward pass, the gradient of the score with respect to every weight. So we hold the full compass. But knowing where to descend is not descending. How far to step each time? Gradient descent, in chapter nine, turns this gradient into a weight-update rule, and finally makes the network learn.
