Chapter eleven. Weight initialisation and batch normalisation. Starting from the right point, at the right scale, and keeping that scale layer after layer.

In chapter nine, we proved that gradient descent drives the score down step by step, wherever it starts from. In chapter ten, every regularisation pulled the weights towards zero. But one question was carefully avoided: where do we start from, exactly? Chapter ten ended on a warning. Starting from zero is fatal, and starting too large is too. So the question of this chapter is this. Which weights should we give a network before it has seen a single example? We will prove why they must all be different, compute the exact scale they must have, then see how a very simple operation, batch normalisation, keeps that scale throughout training.

Act one, the twins. Picture two students who, from the very first day of the year, each copy the other's paper. They hand in the same paper, receive the same correction, draw the same lesson from it, and hand in the same paper again for the next assignment. By the end of the year, the class has two students but only one brain. Two hidden neurons that start with exactly the same weights live the same story. They have the same forward pass, so the same output. The output error signal is the same for every hidden neuron, and each one carries it back through an identical weight, then modulates it by an identical slope. They therefore receive the same gradients, and the update leaves them equal. We prove it by induction: if the equality holds at step t, it holds at step t plus one, and it holds at step zero by assumption. So it holds forever, whatever the examples.

What this costs is considerable. Two twins behave like a single neuron whose output weight has been doubled. If all the hidden neurons start identical, the whole layer behaves like a single neuron. And a single hidden neuron cannot solve XOR, since its decision boundary is a line, and chapter four proved that no line separates the two classes of XOR. Starting from zero is even worse: the neurons are twins, and on top of that a zero output weight cancels their error signal, so at the first step the hidden layer does not move at all. The remedy is to draw the starting weights at random: two neurons that do not start from the same point do not receive the same gradients, and each can specialise. This is symmetry breaking. The biases, for their part, can start at zero, because as soon as the weights differ, the symmetry is already broken.

In the lab, you can train a network with two hidden neurons on the four points of XOR, starting either from two twins or from weights drawn at random. With twins, the two lines stay superimposed from the first step to the last and the score gets stuck. With randomness, they move apart, and XOR is solved within a few hundred steps, after a plateau at the start of the run.

Act two, the right scale. We know the weights must be different, but how large? Think of a chain of amplifiers, like a concert sound system. If each stage multiplies the volume by one point one, after fifty stages the sound is about one hundred and seventeen times louder. If it multiplies it by zero point nine, it is nearly two hundred times quieter. A deep network is a chain of this kind: each layer multiplies the size of the signal by a factor that depends on its weights, and to cross fifty layers without exploding or dying out, that factor must equal one. Too small, and the signal dies out, and the gradient with it. Too large, and it explodes, the pre-activations move far from zero into the saturation zone of the sigmoids, where the slope is almost zero, and we fall back on the vanishing gradient of chapter eight.

To measure the size of a random signal, we use variance: it is the mean of the square of the distance to the mean. Its square root is the standard deviation. We need three properties. A centred quantity, with mean zero, has as its variance the mean of its square. Changing the scale by a factor a multiplies the variance by a squared. And if two quantities are independent, the mean of their product is the product of their means. With these, we prove the central lemma: for a weighted sum of n terms, with centred, independent weights of the same variance, the variance of the sum equals n times the variance of one weight, times the mean square of the inputs. The proof expands the square of the sum: every cross term has zero mean, because the weights are centred and independent, and only the n diagonal terms remain. Note carefully that the inputs do not need to be centred. This detail will matter in the next act.

Layer after layer, with a centred activation that looks like the identity near zero, such as the hyperbolic tangent, the variance is multiplied by n times the variance of one weight at each layer. This is exactly the chain of amplifiers. For this factor to equal one, the variance of the weights must equal one over n, which is the recommendation of LeCun and his co-authors in nineteen ninety-eight. With weights of variance one and a hundred inputs, the factor would be a hundred per layer. But the way back matters too: on the way up, the error signal is a sum over the neurons of the next layer, so we would need one over the number of outputs. The two requirements coincide only if the layer has as many inputs as outputs. Glorot and Bengio propose a compromise, Xavier initialisation: a variance equal to two over the sum of the number of inputs and the number of outputs.

A word on the sigmoid. This whole argument assumes a centred activation, and the sigmoid is not, since its outputs are all positive, around one half. On top of that, its slope never exceeds one quarter, so the error signal is divided by at least four at each layer climbed. This is one of the reasons it left the hidden layers, in favour of the hyperbolic tangent and then the ReLU.

Act three, the ReLU and the factor two. The ReLU is the default activation of deep networks, but it is neither centred nor the identity near zero, since it wipes out the entire negative half. The previous argument therefore does not apply as it stands. But the lemma does, since it demands nothing of the inputs: we only need to compute their mean square. If the pre-activation is symmetric, the ReLU keeps exactly half of its square: the mean of the square of the ReLU of z equals one half of the variance of z. The proof splits the square according to the sign of z, and by symmetry, the two pieces are equal.

We combine the two lemmas, and this time the equality is exact: the variance of the next layer equals n, times the variance of one weight, times one half, times the variance of the previous layer. For the factor to equal one, the weight variance must be two over the number of inputs. This is He initialisation, named after Kaiming He and his co-authors, in twenty fifteen. The factor two exactly compensates for the half of the signal the ReLU wipes out. In numbers, with ten layers of sixty-four neurons and weights of variance one, the signal is multiplied by millions between the first and the tenth layer. With Xavier, the standard deviation is divided by the square root of two at each layer. With He, it stays exactly the same.

In the lab, switch to depth mode: it draws a network of ten layers, and displays, layer by layer, the standard deviation of the activations and the size of the gradient, on a logarithmic scale. You will see the exponential explosion in ReLU with weights of variance one, the steady decline with Xavier, the flat line with He, and the signal dying out in tanh with tiny weights.

Act four, batch normalisation. A good initialisation sets the scale at the start. But training modifies the weights, and at every step, the distribution of the inputs of each layer shifts: a deep layer learns on ground that keeps moving under its feet. In twenty fifteen, Ioffe and Szegedy proposed putting the scale back in order at every layer, at every step. Think of an exam moderation panel that harmonises the marks of several examiners: a strict examiner marks everything at half, another, generous one adds three points everywhere, and after harmonisation each paper regains its relative place, whichever examiner marked it.

Concretely, for one neuron, we take its pre-activations over the examples of the mini-batch, compute their mean and variance, then subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation, with a tiny epsilon so as never to divide by zero. We then apply two parameters learned by gradient descent, gamma, which scales, and beta, which shifts. The result of this computation is what goes through the activation.

Three exact properties. One, over the batch, the normalised values have mean zero and a variance almost equal to one, because the sum of the distances to the mean is zero. Two, scale invariance: if we replace every pre-activation by a times itself plus c, with a positive, the normalised values do not change, because the mean and the standard deviation follow exactly the same change. In other words, multiplying all the incoming weights of a neuron no longer shows after normalisation, and its bias is erased too: it is beta that takes over its role. Three, nothing is lost: if gamma equals the standard deviation of the batch and beta its mean, we recover exactly the original pre-activation. The layer can therefore undo the normalisation if that is what lowers the score, and normalisation removes no expressive power. To predict a single example, there is no batch: we use a running average of the means and variances met during training.

In the lab, switch back to depth mode with normalisation on: the standard deviation bars are identical whatever the scale of the weights, the gradients regain a reasonable size, and the choice of initialisation scheme hardly matters any more.

One last nuance. The fact is solid: batch normalisation speeds up training a great deal, and allows larger learning rates. The explanation, however, is debated. Ioffe and Szegedy attributed it to internal covariate shift, but Santurkar and his co-authors showed that training stays just as fast when we deliberately add noise after each normalisation, and suggest instead that the cost surface becomes smoother. A widely used variant, layer normalisation, does not depend on the batch size, and it is the one used in transformers.

The chapter ends with five pencil-and-paper exercises, solved line by line: two twins that take a descent step and remain twins, the variance of a pre-activation that is too large and the sigmoid saturation that follows, the Xavier and He values on one specific layer, ten ReLU layers with Xavier and then with He, and a batch normalisation by hand on four values.

In one sentence. Identical starting weights stay identical forever, so we draw them at random, with a variance of two over the sum of inputs and outputs for Xavier, or two over the inputs for the ReLU with He, so that the signal neither dies out nor explodes from one layer to the next, and batch normalisation maintains that scale throughout training by making each layer insensitive to the scale of its weights.

Towards chapter twelve. The network now starts from the right point, at the right scale, and batch normalisation keeps it there. But all its weights still advance at the same pace, that of a single learning rate. Yet chapter nine showed the trap of the ravine: a score that is very steep in one direction and very flat in the other. A single step for everyone is too large for the steep direction, where the descent zigzags from one edge to the other, and too small for the flat direction, where it takes hundreds of steps to move forward. Chapter twelve brings two ideas: giving the descent some inertia to damp the zigzag, which is momentum, and giving each weight its own step, which is RMSprop and then Adam.
