Chapter three. Sets and operations. How membership, inclusion and three operations turn the connectives of chapter one into objects you can manipulate.

In chapter two, we spent all our time talking about the domain of discourse, the set over which variables range, while settling for an intuitive idea: a collection of objects. The chapter even closed on a promise: A included in B would be read, for all x, if x belongs to A then x belongs to B. It is time to keep it. But what is a set, exactly? The answer is lovelier than a mere catalogue of symbols. You are about to discover that set theory is not a new territory to memorise: it is the logic of chapter one, poured into objects. The or becomes union, the and becomes intersection, the not becomes complement, and the De Morgan laws you already know will reappear, identical, on sets.

Describing a set.

A set is a collection of objects, its elements, regarded as a single whole. The basic gesture, the one everything else flows from, is to ask whether an object is in or out. This relation is called membership. x belongs to A means that x is one of the elements of A. Notice that x belongs to A is exactly a predicate in the sense of chapter two: depending on what we put in place of x, it is true or false. We describe a set in two ways. By extension, we list the elements in braces, for example one, two, three. By comprehension, we give the property the elements satisfy, for example the set of all x such that x is positive and smaller than four. And this second form reveals the deep link: a set by comprehension is a predicate turned into an object, the collection of all the witnesses that make it true. One limit case earns its own name: the set with no element, the empty set. It is not nothing, it is a perfectly legitimate set, just empty. Finally, a founding principle: a set is entirely determined by its elements. Order and repetitions do not count. One, two, three and three, two, one are the same set.

Comparing: inclusion and equality.

The promise of chapter two arrives here. Inclusion, A included in B, says that every element of A is also in B. And its definition is a quantified statement: A included in B is equivalent to, for all x, if x belongs to A then x belongs to B. So inclusion is not one more symbol to memorise, it is a universal implication. A surprising case confirms it: the empty set is included in every set. Why? It would require that, for all x, if x belongs to the empty set then x belongs to B. But x belongs to the empty set is always false, and in chapter one, an implication with a false premise is always true. The false implies true that surprised everyone has a concrete consequence here: the empty set is included everywhere. How then do we say two sets are equal? Each is included in the other. We call this double inclusion, and it is the most used proof tool whenever we want to prove an equality of sets: we prove one inclusion, then the other. We can also gather all the subsets of a set E into a new set, the power set of E. Its elements are themselves sets. If E has n elements, its power set has two to the power n, because to build a subset, each element is either taken or left out.

Combining: the operations are the connectives.

Here is the heart of the chapter. From two sets, we build new ones with three operations, and each is defined by its membership condition, which is a connective from chapter one. Union, A union B, gathers: an object is in it if it is in A or in B. Union is the logical or turned into an object. Intersection, A inter B, keeps only the common part: an object is in it if it is in A and in B. Intersection is the and. The complement of A returns what is not in A, relative to a reference universe: an object is in it if not, it belongs to A. This is the not. Careful, the complement only makes sense if the universe is fixed, exactly like the domain of discourse of chapter two. And since the operations are connectives, the laws that govern the connectives become laws on sets. The De Morgan laws of chapter one transpose word for word: the complement of a union is the intersection of the complements, and the complement of an intersection is the union of the complements. To not be in A or B, you must be neither in A nor in B. You are not proving a new truth, you are recognising an old one in a new costume.

On the page, a laboratory makes all this tangible. The universe runs from one to six, starting with A equals one, two, three, and B equals three, four, five. You place each element in one of the four regions by clicking on it, A only, the intersection, B only, or outside both, and you choose the operation to display. Then turn on De Morgan mode: on the left, the component computes the complement of A union B, on the right, the intersection of the complement of A and the complement of B. The two lists are identical, here the single element six, outside both circles. Move the elements as much as you like: the two sides always stay equal. You have not proved anything yet, but you have watched the law resist every attempt.

Building anew: the cartesian product.

The three previous operations stay inside the starting universe. One last construction truly steps outside: the cartesian product, written with a cross. A cross B is the set of all ordered pairs whose first coordinate comes from A and second from B. Order matters: the pair a, b is not the pair b, a. And the count is clear: if A has m elements and B has n, then A cross B has m times n, hence the name. Remember where this brick leads: a pair is the seed of a relation, and a well-chosen relation is a function.

In one sentence.

A set is determined by its elements through membership; inclusion is a universal implication, union an or, intersection an and, complement a not, so the De Morgan laws of chapter one still govern sets, word for word.

Towards chapter four.

You have seen, on the component and on examples, that the complement of a union equals the intersection of the complements. You tested it, again and again, never catching it out. But to test is not to prove: checking an identity on a universe of six elements does not prove it holds for all sets, exactly as trying a hundred numbers does not prove a universal property. How do we pass from it works every time to it is true, always, with no exception? The key is already there: to prove an equality of sets, we prove both inclusions, and each inclusion is a universal implication to prove. Chapter four gives precisely the techniques for that, with no example and no drawing: direct proof, contrapositive, proof by contradiction. On the page, the chapter ends with two exercises solved line by line, proving that a set is included in its union with another, and checking De Morgan on a finite universe, followed by a quiz to anchor it all.
