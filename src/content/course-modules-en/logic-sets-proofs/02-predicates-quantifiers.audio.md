Chapter two. Predicates and quantifiers. How for all and there exists turn an open statement into a truth, and why the order of quantifiers changes everything.

In chapter one, we carefully refused to call x greater than three a proposition, since there is no way to say whether it is true or false until x is fixed. Yet mathematics overflows with statements of this kind. How do we give them a truth value, and therefore the right to enter a proof? The answer comes in two gestures: naming these open statements, the predicates, then closing them with two tiny, formidable words, for all and there exists.

The open statement.

Take x greater than three again. It is not a proposition, but it is not noise either. As soon as you replace x by a number, the sentence becomes true or false. If x is five, it is true; if x is one, it is false. It is a form with a blank to fill in. A predicate is exactly that: a statement containing one or more variables, whose truth value depends on what you put in the blanks. As long as the variable stays free, the predicate is neither true nor false, it waits. There are two ways to push it into the state of a proposition. The first, substitute a value. The second, far more powerful, quantify the variable, that is, say in one stroke something about all possible values, or about at least one.

What the variable ranges over: the domain of discourse.

Before quantifying, a question we cannot dodge: what does x range over? The integers? The reals? The inhabitants of a city? This reference set is called the domain of discourse, and it is part of the statement just as much as the predicate is. This is not a detail. Consider the statement there exists a number whose square is two. Over the domain of integers, it is false: no integer squared gives two. Over the domain of reals, it is true, since the square root of two exists. Same predicate, same quantifier, opposite truths, and only the domain changed. Remember the rule: a quantified statement with no stated domain is incomplete.

The two quantifiers.

To quantify is to bind the variable: it stops being a free blank and becomes the subject of a global claim. The universal quantifier, written for all, claims that each element of the domain, without exception, satisfies the predicate. For example, over the integers, for all n, n plus zero equals n, is true. But for all n, n greater than three, is false, because n equals two is enough to contradict it. The existential quantifier, written there exists, claims that at least one element satisfies the predicate. Such an element is called a witness. Beware the everyday sense: there exists does not mean there exists a unique. One witness is enough, several are allowed. So, over the integers, there exists n such that n is greater than three, is true, and four is a witness, so is a hundred. Keep the habit of saying the statement out loud in full: for all x, we have P of x. A statement you can say aloud is a statement you have understood.

Negating a quantifier.

How do you refute for all x, P of x? Not by checking every case, that would be endless. A single failure is enough. And symmetrically, negating there exists amounts to saying none, hence all fail. These two rules follow directly from chapter one's De Morgan laws, extended from the finite to the infinite. The negation of for all x, P of x equals there exists x such that not P of x. And the negation of there exists x such that P of x equals for all x, not P of x. The quantifier flips, and the negation slides inward, onto the predicate. This is exactly De Morgan's move, an infinite and becoming an infinite or. From the first rule comes the most economical tool in mathematics. To demolish a universal claim, you exhibit a counter-example: a single element of the domain where the predicate is false. A counter-example does not argue with a conjecture, it kills it. History gives a famous case. Fermat conjectured that all numbers two to the power, two to the power n, plus one, are prime. It was true for n from zero to four. Then Euler, in seventeen thirty-two, computes the case n equals five: two to the power thirty-two, plus one, factors into six hundred forty-one times six million seven hundred thousand four hundred seventeen. A single counter-example, and the for all collapses.

The trap: the order of quantifiers.

Here is the point that trips everyone up, and the true reason for this chapter. When you stack two different quantifiers, their order changes the meaning. Compare two statements. The first: for all x, there exists y, such that R of x, y. It says that for each x, there exists a y, which may depend on x, such that the relation holds. Each one has its partner, possibly different. The second: there exists y, for all x, such that R of x, y. It says there exists one y that works for all x at once. A universal partner, the same for everyone. The analogy that engraves the difference: everyone has a mother, that is true; there exists a mother of everyone, that is false, thankfully. Same words, reversed order, opposite truths. On the page, a small lab lets you edit a relation on a grid: a checked cell means x is linked to y. It starts on the diagonal, where each one is linked to itself and to no one else. Leave the statement on for all x, there exists y, and read the verdict: it is true, because each one has a witness, itself. Then click swap the axes to move to there exists y, for all x, without changing anything else: the verdict becomes false, because no y is linked to all x. The same table, two truths. One rule to finish: you can always slide a there exists leftward, above a for all, but never the other way round. A universal partner serves everyone, so each one has a partner; but each having their own does not manufacture a common partner.

In one sentence.

A predicate is an open statement; quantifying it with for all or there exists over a stated domain closes it into a true-or-false proposition, its negation swaps the two quantifiers, and their order is never innocent.

Towards chapter three.

We kept talking about the domain of discourse, the set the variables range over, settling for an intuitive idea: a collection of objects. But what is a set, exactly? How do we describe one, how do we test whether an object belongs to it, how do we build new ones from old? The quantifiers of this chapter will become the very language of that construction: A included in B will read for all x, if x belongs to A then x belongs to B. That is all of chapter three, set theory. On the page, the chapter ends with two solutions worked line by line and a quiz, to anchor all this.
